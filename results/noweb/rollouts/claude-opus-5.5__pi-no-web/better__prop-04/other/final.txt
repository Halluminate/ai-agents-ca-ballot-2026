I can't give a one-word answer here because it depends on your values, and I don't take sides on ballot measures. Here's a quick breakdown to help you decide.

**What a yes vote does:** It lifts the ban voters approved in 1988, so the state, counties, and cities *may* create public campaign-financing programs, such as matching small donations. It doesn't create any program or spend campaign money by itself. Each government would decide separately.

**Supporters argue:**
- It reduces candidates' dependence on wealthy donors and special interests.
- It helps people without wealthy networks run competitive campaigns.
- Each community gets to decide, and funds for education, transportation, and public safety are off-limits.

**Opponents argue:**
- Taxpayer money would fund political campaigns, including candidates you may oppose.
- Money is fungible, so even with the earmark protections, campaign funding competes with other budget needs.
- These programs add administrative costs and may not meaningfully reduce special-interest influence.

**In short:** If you see big money in politics as a major problem and want governments to have this option, you'd likely lean yes. If you object to tax dollars funding campaigns or doubt these programs are worth the cost, you'd likely lean no.
