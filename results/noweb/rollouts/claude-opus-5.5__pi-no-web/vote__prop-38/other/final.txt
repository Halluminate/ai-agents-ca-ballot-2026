I won't give a yes or no here, because I don't think it's my place to tell you how to vote. This one really comes down to your priorities. Here's the decision in a nutshell, based on the summary you shared.

**Case for Yes**
- It funds immunotherapy research, a field behind major cancer breakthroughs that's now being explored for autoimmune and neurological diseases. Patient-advocacy and nursing groups back it.
- It gives California a large, steady funding source that doesn't depend on federal research budgets, and it could attract scientists and biotech jobs.
- The annual cost is well under 1% of the state budget, and some of it could be recouped if the research generates revenue.

**Case for No**
- Repayment would total roughly $10–12 billion ($8.4 billion plus interest) over about 20 years from the general fund, competing with other priorities.
- Half the money ($4.2 billion) goes to a single institute. That's unusual and raises questions about competition and oversight.
- The revenue offset is speculative. Direct financial returns from state research programs have historically been modest.

**A useful precedent:** California's stem cell agency (CIRM) was funded by similar bonds in 2004 and 2020. It has funded many clinical trials, but critics say it overpromised cures and had conflict-of-interest problems on its board. How you view that track record is a decent guide here.

**The decision mostly comes down to three questions:**
- Is borrowing a good way to fund medical research?
- Are you comfortable with the oversight of the single-institute half? The full text and arguments in the official voter guide will tell you more.
- Do the potential health benefits justify the long-term cost?
