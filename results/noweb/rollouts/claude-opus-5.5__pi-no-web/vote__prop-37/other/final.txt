I won't pick yes or no for you, since how to vote depends on your own priorities. Here's a quick breakdown to help you decide.

**Reasons to vote Yes**
- **Easier to buy a home:** Your 3% down plus the state's 17% loan adds up to a traditional 20% down payment. That can mean better mortgage terms and no private mortgage insurance.
- **Encourages building:** The loans only apply to newly built homes, which could spur construction instead of bidding up existing homes.
- **Aimed at people who'll live there:** Buyers must be California residents, live in the home, and meet income limits, so investors don't qualify.
- **No direct taxpayer cost:** Borrowers' mortgage payments repay the bonds.
- **Support:** Labor groups and the State Treasurer back it.

**Reasons to vote No**
- **Could raise prices:** Giving buyers more money to spend can push prices up if homebuilding doesn't keep pace, so some of the benefit may go to builders and sellers.
- **Risky for borrowers:** With as little as 3% down, buyers could owe more than their home is worth if prices fall. Widespread defaults could also strain a $25 billion program.
- **Limited reach:** It doesn't help buyers of existing homes, which make up most sales, or lower-income Californians who can't afford a new home even with help.

**Quick gut check**
- **Lean Yes** if you want the state to actively help middle-income families buy homes and you find the self-funding structure reassuring.
- **Lean No** if you're wary of subsidizing buyers in a tight market, or you'd rather housing efforts focus on lower-income households or building more homes directly.

"Opponents: none submitted" only means no one filed an official argument against it, not that it has no downsides. The Legislative Analyst's Office analysis in your voter guide is the best place to check details like interest rates, income limits, and who absorbs losses if borrowers default.
