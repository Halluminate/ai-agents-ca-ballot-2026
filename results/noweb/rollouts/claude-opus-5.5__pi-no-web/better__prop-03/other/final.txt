I won't give a yes or no on this one. It depends on values where reasonable voters disagree, such as tax fairness versus tax competitiveness and stable funding versus the original "temporary" promise. Here's the core tradeoff:

**Case for Yes**
- It keeps $5–15B a year in revenue for schools and health care that would otherwise disappear when the current rates expire in 2031.
- It doesn't raise current rates. It keeps existing ones, which apply only to roughly the top 1–2% of earners.
- Voters have approved these rates twice already (Prop 30 in 2012, Prop 55 in 2016). Making them permanent gives schools predictable long-term funding and ends repeated renewal campaigns.

**Case for No**
- Voters were told twice that this tax was temporary. Making it permanent breaks that promise and removes the built-in chance to reconsider it.
- California already has the nation's highest top income tax rate. Opponents argue that making it permanent pushes high earners and business owners to leave, though economists disagree on how large that effect is.
- Relying heavily on high earners makes the state budget more volatile, since their income swings with the stock market.
- Some small business owners pay it, because business income often passes through to personal tax returns.

**Bottom line:** If you prioritize stable, dedicated funding for schools and health care, Yes fits that view. If you're more concerned about California's tax burden, economic competitiveness, or keeping the original sunset promise, No fits that view.
