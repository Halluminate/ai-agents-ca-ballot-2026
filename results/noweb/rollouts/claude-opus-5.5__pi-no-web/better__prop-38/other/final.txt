I'd rather not tell you how to vote. It depends on how you weigh the tradeoffs, but here's a quick breakdown:

**Case for yes**
- It funds immunology and immunotherapy research that could advance treatments for cancer, autoimmune disease, Alzheimer's, and more.
- Several patient-advocacy and health groups support it.
- It could bring research jobs and investment to California, and revenue from discoveries could offset some of the cost.

**Case for no**
- It costs $500–600 million a year for about 20 years, roughly $10–12 billion total. That money would compete with schools, health care, and other state priorities.
- Half the money ($4.2 billion) goes to a single institute, which raises questions about concentration and oversight.
- It borrows to pay for work usually funded by the federal government and private industry, and whether it will generate offsetting revenue is uncertain.

**How to decide:** If you see state-funded medical research as a worthwhile long-term investment and accept the debt, lean yes. If you're wary of state borrowing or of sending billions to one institution, lean no. California's earlier stem-cell research bonds (Props 71 and 14) are a useful precedent, and your view of that program's record may help you decide.
