I'll skip the one-word answer because how to vote on a ballot measure is your call, but here's a quick rundown to help you decide.

**Case for Yes**
- **Easier to buy with little down:** With 3% down and a 17% program loan, the main mortgage only needs to cover 80%, which typically avoids private mortgage insurance.
- **Targets new construction:** Because it's limited to newly built homes, it could expand the pool of buyers for new projects and encourage more building rather than bidding up existing homes.
- **No direct taxpayer cost:** The bonds are repaid by borrowers' mortgage payments, not the state.
- **Broad support:** Nurses' and carpenters' unions and the State Treasurer back it. No official opposition argument was filed, though that doesn't mean it has no critics.

**Case for No**
- **May raise prices:** When housing supply is tight, buyer subsidies can be partly absorbed into higher prices, which benefits builders more than buyers.
- **Who it helps:** With a price cap near $1.5M, it may reach fairly well-off households, depending on the income limits. It does little for renters, lower-income families, or buyers of existing homes.
- **Financial risk:** Borrowing 97% of the price leaves buyers little equity. If prices fall, borrowers can end up owing more than the home is worth, and the program's loans, likely second in line behind the main mortgage, would take losses first.
- **Doesn't fix root causes:** Critics argue the real constraints are zoning, permitting, and construction costs, not financing.

**Bottom line:** Lean yes if your priority is helping middle-income families buy new homes and possibly spurring construction. Lean no if you're more worried it will mainly push up prices or add financial risk without increasing supply.

The official voter guide has specifics like the income limits, interest rate, and repayment terms on the 17% loan.
