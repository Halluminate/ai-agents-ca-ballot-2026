Whether to vote "yes" or "no" on Proposition 1 depends on how voters weigh the need for state-funded affordable housing against the long-term fiscal cost of issuing state bonds. According to the California Official Voter Information Guide and the Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO), the measure would authorize $11.25 billion in general obligation bonds to fund a variety of housing programs.

### Arguments in Favor of Voting "Yes"
According to supporters—including U.S. VETS, Habitat for Humanity California, the California Federation of Teachers, Self-Help for the Elderly, and the California Alliance of Child and Family Services:

* **Expanding Affordable Housing:** Proponents argue that the measure provides vital funding to address the state's severe housing shortage by financing the construction and preservation of affordable multifamily rental housing.
* **Targeted Assistance:** Supporters emphasize that the bond dedicates critical resources to supportive housing for vulnerable populations, mortgage assistance for veterans, and downpayment aid for working families seeking homeownership.
* **Addressing Homelessness:** Advocacy groups contend that increasing the supply of subsidized and supportive housing is essential to reducing homelessness and relieving cost burdens on low- and middle-income residents.

### Arguments in Favor of Voting "No"
While no formal argument against the measure was submitted for the ballot pamphlet, fiscal watchdogs, taxpayer advocacy groups, and critics of state bond financing often raise several concerns:

* **Long-Term Debt Costs:** Based on the Legislative Analyst’s fiscal estimate, repaying the bond with interest would cost taxpayers between $500 million and $600 million annually over approximately 25 years, totaling significantly more than the initial principal borrowed.
* **Impact on the General Fund:** Opponents of bond funding argue that servicing bond debt creates a fixed obligation paid out of the state’s General Fund, which could limit available funding for other public services, education, or infrastructure during budget downturns.
* **Approach to Housing Affordability:** Critics often argue that subsidizing housing through debt does not address the underlying causes of high housing prices, such as local zoning restrictions, environmental review delays, and high construction costs, and contend that regulatory reforms should be prioritized over public borrowing.
