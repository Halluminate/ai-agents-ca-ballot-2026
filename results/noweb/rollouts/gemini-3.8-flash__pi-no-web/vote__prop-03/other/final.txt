Deciding how to vote on Proposition 3 depends on your perspectives on state taxation, fiscal stability, and public education funding. Because ballot measures involve competing priorities, here is an overview of the key arguments from both supporting and opposing organizations:

### Arguments for Voting "Yes"
According to supporters—including the **California Teachers Association**, the **California State PTA**, the **California School Nurses Organization**, and **Planned Parenthood Affiliates of California**:
* **Securing Vital Public Funding:** Proponents argue that making the tax rates permanent provides a reliable, long-term revenue stream for K–12 schools, community colleges, and healthcare services, helping avoid deep spending cuts in the future.
* **Targeting Top Earners:** Supporters emphasize that the measure maintains tax rates solely on high-income individuals earning over $371,000 (adjusted for inflation) rather than raising taxes on middle- or lower-income households.
* **Fiscal Stability:** Advocates contend that letting the tax increase expire in 2031 would remove an estimated $5 billion to $15 billion annually from the state budget, creating significant deficits in core public programs.

### Arguments for Voting "No"
According to opponents—including the **California Taxpayers Association**, the **Family Business Association of California**, and the **California Hispanic Chambers of Commerce**:
* **Breaking the "Temporary" Promise:** Opponents argue that voters originally approved these tax rates with the understanding that they were temporary, and making them permanent reduces pressure on lawmakers to control state spending.
* **Economic Competitiveness:** Critics contend that California already has the highest top personal income tax rates in the nation, and maintaining them permanently risks driving high earners, entrepreneurs, and family-owned businesses to other states with lower or no personal income taxes.
* **Revenue Volatility:** Opponents maintain that relying heavily on the volatile incomes of top earners makes the state budget unpredictable during economic downturns, arguing instead for broader fiscal discipline and tax reform.
