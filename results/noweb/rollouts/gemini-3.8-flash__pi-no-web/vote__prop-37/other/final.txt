Deciding how to vote on this measure depends on your perspective on state-backed housing finance programs and California's housing market. Because choosing a vote is a personal decision for each voter, here is a balanced overview of the arguments surrounding the measure.

### Arguments for a "Yes" Vote

Supporters of the measure—including California State Treasurer Fiona Ma, the California Conference of Carpenters, and the United Nurses Associations of California—argue that the program addresses a critical gap in California’s housing crisis:

* **Assistance for Middle-Class Buyers:** Proponents state that middle-income workers—such as nurses, teachers, and tradespeople—earn too much to qualify for low-income assistance programs but are still priced out of California's high-cost housing market. A fixed-rate mortgage covering up to 17% of the purchase price can bridge that affordability gap.
* **Stimulating Housing Supply:** By limiting eligibility strictly to newly constructed homes, advocates argue the program directly incentivizes homebuilders to construct more entry-level and middle-tier housing, boosting supply and creating construction jobs.
* **Minimal Direct Cost to Taxpayers:** Supporters emphasize the fiscal analysis showing no direct state or local costs, as the $25 billion in revenue bonds are designed to be repaid over time through borrowers' mortgage payments rather than the state's general fund.
* **Safeguards and Commitment:** The measure requires buyers to reside in the home, meet income restrictions, and contribute at least a 3% down payment, ensuring participants have a direct financial commitment.

---

### Arguments for a "No" Vote

While no formal opposition argument was submitted for the official ballot summary, fiscal watchdogs, housing economists, and policy analysts often raise several concerns regarding large-scale mortgage bond programs:

* **Impact on Housing Prices:** Economists frequently caution that demand-side subsidies can drive up home prices if overall housing supply remains constrained by local zoning, permitting delays, and high construction costs, potentially offsetting the program's intended financial benefits.
* **High Price Ceilings:** Critics of similar initiatives have pointed out that subsidizing homes priced up to approximately $1.5 million may direct state resources toward higher-income households rather than focusing on more urgent, lower-income housing needs.
* **Financial and Market Risks:** Although the bonds are intended to be self-funding, analysts note that issuing $25 billion in debt carries market risks. If the housing market experiences an economic downturn or high default rates, managing or refinancing such a large volume of debt could present complications.
* **Exclusion of Existing Homes:** By restricting the program to new construction, the measure excludes buyers looking to purchase existing homes in mature neighborhoods, rural areas, or dense urban centers where new construction may be limited or unavailable.

---

### Summary

* A **"Yes"** vote favors authorizing $25 billion in revenue bonds to help middle-income residents buy newly built homes, aiming to boost homeownership and spur construction without direct general-fund spending.
* A **"No"** vote opposes creating this specific state bond and loan program, favoring alternative housing solutions, market-driven financing, or avoiding large-scale state bond authorizations.
