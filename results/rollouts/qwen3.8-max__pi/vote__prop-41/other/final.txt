**No.**

**What Prop 41 actually does (Nov. 3, 2026 ballot):** It adds Article XXIV to the state Constitution to (1) require the State Auditor to run pre‑election performance/financial audits of programs that would be funded by any initiative special tax (triggered at 25% of signatures), with a mandate to recommend ≥10% annual cost cuts and comparisons to "analogous programs in the nongovernment sector," printed in the Voter Guide right after the LAO's impartial analysis; (2) require audits every four years of programs funded by special taxes enacted on/after Jan. 1, 2026; and (3) bar the state from imposing, collecting, or enforcing **any** tax enacted or taking effect on/after Jan. 1, 2026 that is excluded from the Gann Limit — including via a statutory appropriations-limit increase under Art. XIII B §4.

**Why I'm voting no:**

- **It's a concealed nullification device.** Its conflict clause voids *all* provisions of any same-ballot measure containing a Gann-excluded tax if Prop 41 gets more "yes" votes — killing Prop 40 even if a majority approves it, and potentially Prop 3 (the education millionaire's tax, which carries different audit rules). The title, summary, and yes-argument never mention this. If you oppose the billionaire tax, you can vote no on Prop 40 directly; you don't need to permanently rewrite the Constitution to do it.
- **Serious, well-documented collateral damage.** Because §4(b)(2) bans appropriations-limit increases by statute, tax-law scholars (Galle/Gamage/Shanske, Aug. 2026 memo) and the nonpartisan California Budget & Policy Center warn Prop 41 could jeopardize the MCO tax, Hospital Quality Assurance Fee, and SNF fee — mechanisms that draw down roughly $8+ billion/yr in federal Medi-Cal matching funds — plus SB 125's Medi-Cal Stability Fund (enacted June 2026). It also hands opponents of *any* future tax initiative a new single-subject litigation weapon, since escaping Prop 41 would require bundling a constitutional override with a tax.
- **The Gann lock is bad policy, not accountability.** The 1979 cap grows only with population + inflation. Voters could approve a dedicated tax and find the state legally unable to spend it on the intended purpose when it's near the cap — excess goes to rebates/schools instead. The League of Women Voters opposed Gann in 1979 for this inflexibility and opposes Prop 41; SPUR, CA Budget & Policy Center, CA Democratic Party, and the SF Chronicle, Sacramento Bee, LA Times, and Mercury News editorial boards all say no. (Notably, outlets that also oppose Prop 40 still say no on 41 — calling it a "cynical poison pill.")
- **The audits are redundant and tilted.** California already has an independent State Auditor with high-risk, legislative-request, and whistleblower audit authority; Prop 30/3 revenues are already audited. The new regime is prescriptive (arbitrary 10% savings target, private-sector benchmarking), costs the General Fund when initiatives fail, and can require auditing programs that don't yet exist — a chilling tax on the citizen initiative itself.
- **Money and motive:** ~$58M to the yes committee, nearly all from Building a Better California ($102M from Sergey Brin alone; $186M across Props 41/42), which also spent ~$118M against Prop 40.

The one legitimate idea here — more performance auditing before new taxes — is already achievable through the State Auditor and Legislature, without a permanent constitutional cap-lock, without a hidden poison pill, and without risking billions in federal health funding.
