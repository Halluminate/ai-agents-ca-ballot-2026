## Vote: **NO**

I researched this extensively — the official VIG, the LAO analysis, the full statutory text, campaign finance filings, CalMatters' investigative reporting, UC Regents testimony, polling, and endorsements from across the spectrum. The policy *goal* (immunotherapy research) is legitimate. The *instrument* is one of the worst-constructed measures I've seen on a California ballot. Here's what I verified firsthand.

**1. Half the money is pre-awarded to a beneficiary the text never names.** Section 130353(a) sets eligibility at: a UC-affiliated nonprofit founded before 1/1/2025, tied to a health system with **>35,000 inpatient admissions and >3,500,000 outpatient visits**, ≥200,000 sq ft, ≥$250M philanthropic funding, plus ≥$50M for a "**rapid vaccine development program**" and ≥$50M for a "**microbiome research program**." Per UC's own fiscal audit, only UCLA clears 3.5M visits (4.1M); UCSF is second at 3.4M. Those last two criteria match, nearly verbatim, two $50M gifts from Gary and Alya Michelson. A UCLA spokesperson confirmed only one entity qualifies. The Yes campaign's own spokesperson conceded: "It is very likely that CIII... will qualify."

**2. The measure's chief funder chairs the institute that receives the money.** SOS filings through 10/22/26: **$71,229,216 in support vs. $11,990 in opposition** — roughly 6,000:1. About $61.2M is Michelson (≈$14M structured as *loans*); $5M+ from co-founder Meyer Luskin. Michelson is CIII's co-founder and **board chair**. This is a conflict of interest on the face of the statute.

**3. The recipient is explicitly exempt from California's transparency and ethics laws.** Section 130363(a), verbatim: *"the institute shall not be deemed a public agency for any purpose under state law, including... Division 10 (commencing with Section 7920.000)... Article 9 (commencing with Section 11120)... and Title 9 (commencing with Section 81000)."* That's the **Public Records Act, the Brown Act, and the Political Reform Act** — stripped from a private nonprofit receiving $4.2B in taxpayer money. The pro argument's "strong accountability" claim rests on financial audits the institute itself commissions (§130361(a)). UC's general counsel could not tell the Regents where public/private documents diverge, given four senior UCLA officials sit on CIII's board.

**4. The institute had three employees.** Per UCLA Health CEO John Mazziotta at the Sept. 16 Regents meeting: a COO, a general counsel, a scientific advisor — **still searching for a director**. Roughly $20M spent against a plan calling for $45M operating + $167M capital; construction ~$100M underfunded; facility not complete until **late 2028**. May 2026 state filings showed zero employees. Meanwhile §130353(e)(1) immediately appropriates a **$100M General Fund start-up loan** — before a single bond is sold.

**5. The "competitive" half isn't competitive, and the institute controls everyone else's IP.** Grants go only to institutions "represented on the council" (§130352(b)(2)) — a closed pool of 7 UC campuses plus 10–15 members those 7 appoint. UC Merced, UCSB, UCSC, all of Cal State, and every non-council institution are excluded. The first chair is statutorily the UCLA rep (§130355(d)(2)). The council "shall confer with the institute" on final funding decisions (§130356(e)(2)). Every grantee must offer the institute participation rights (§130357(d)) and **UCLA by name** clinical trial rights (§130357(e)). And §130358(e)(2) makes the institute "a clearinghouse and approval center for **all** agreements to sell, license, or otherwise commercialize" IP from the entire program.

**6. Conflict-of-interest protections are weakened, not strengthened.** §130363(b)(1) lets council members vote grants "to an entity in the same field as their employer"; (b)(2) permits voting on diseases a member or family member has; and (d) provides that **Government Code §1090 — California's core anti-self-dealing statute — "shall not apply."**

**7. The official Yes argument makes a claim the Legislative Analyst contradicts.** It says the measure is "Designed to ensure no cost to taxpayers." The LAO's official estimate: **$500–600M annually for about 20 years** from the General Fund, ~10% more than cash (inflation-adjusted), with revenue "uncertain" and offsets possibly "tak[ing] decades." The debt is guaranteed; the royalties are speculative. California already ran this experiment — Props 71 and 14, $8.5B in near-identical "self-repaying" research bonds. CIRM's audited FY2024 financials show the Stem Cell Licensing Revenue and Royalties Fund collected **$677,205**. Twenty years in, essentially nothing has been recovered.

**8. The "20% Rx discount" is misleading as advertised.** Per §130358(b), it applies only to drugs *developed through this specific program* — a handful of products, decades out. It is not a general prescription-drug discount for Californians.

**9. A better version is already 16 months away.** SB 895 (Wiener), signed by Newsom, places a **$7.5B** research bond on the **March 2028** ballot — multi-field, peer-reviewed, competitively awarded, with transparency protections. Opponent Robert Kaplan, former NIH associate director, says it "has the kinds of transparency protections that I think taxpayers would expect." Also: using 20-year GO debt to fund operating research grants means paying interest on salaries and reagents.

**On the Yes side, fairly:** the Trump administration's attacks on NIH funding are real and California research is genuinely hurting; immunotherapy is genuinely promising; the Michael J. Fox Foundation, Alzheimer's Association, ALS Association, and ANA/CA are sincere supporters; and $500–600M is only ~0.25% of the General Fund. But it's a *continuous appropriation* (§130379) competing with Medi-Cal, schools, housing and wildfire response for two decades, **incremental to** the ~$587M+/yr Californians already pay on CIRM debt. And notably, the UC system itself — invoked throughout the text — took **no position**.

Twelve editorial boards recommend No (SF Chronicle called it "one of the most noxious deposits on California's political dung heap in years"; Mercury News, "an $8.4 billion money grab"; plus LA Times' Skelton, Sacramento Bee/McClatchy, SD Union-Tribune, OC Register, Press Democrat). Opposition spans left and right: League of Women Voters, Common Cause, Consumer Watchdog, Courage California, Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, DSA, ACCE Action, Peace and Freedom Party, and three former Legislative Analysts. Several cancer survivors are leading the No campaign, saying their illnesses are being "exploited to shill for an initiative that is overly narrow, opaque and expensive."

The 57% poll support reflects a ~$20M ad buy, not informed consent — the opposition had raised $11,990.

**Voting No isn't voting against curing cancer. It's voting against handing $4.2 billion, exempt from the Public Records Act, to a three-employee nonprofit chaired by the man who paid to put the measure on the ballot — while the official argument tells you it costs nothing.**

# Vote **NO** on Proposition 38.
