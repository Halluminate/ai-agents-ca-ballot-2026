## Yes

*(Note: the link you gave is the **November 3, 2026** Prop 5 — recall elections — not the 2024 Prop 5 about housing bonds.)*

**What it does:** Removes the "who replaces them?" second question from state recall ballots. A recalled Governor is succeeded by the (separately, statewide-elected) Lieutenant Governor — unless the recall happens in the first two years of the term, in which case voters pick a successor in a special election that requires a **majority** with a top-two runoff. Legislators get a special election; other constitutional officers are filled under existing vacancy rules. Signature thresholds, timelines, and the majority vote to remove are all unchanged.

**Why yes:**

1. **The flaw is real and it has actually fired.** In 2018, Ling Ling Chang won SD‑29 with 50,215 votes (33.8%) while **66,197** people voted to keep Josh Newman — the replacement took office with ~16,000 fewer votes than the incumbent retained. In 2021, Larry Elder would have become Governor with 3,563,867 votes = **27.8% of all recall voters**, while 61.9% voted to keep Newsom. In 2003, Schwarzenegger took office with 4.2M votes — fewer than Gray Davis received in 2002, and only ~45% of ballots cast.
2. **It imposes the majority standard California already uses everywhere else.** A recall is currently the one election where you can become Governor of the world's 4th-largest economy with under 30% support.
3. **It kills a genuine conflict of interest nobody talks about.** Today, Article II §17 puts the **Lieutenant Governor** — the person who personally inherits the governorship if the recall succeeds — in charge of calling and running the gubernatorial recall. Prop 5 moves that duty to the Secretary of State (and the Controller if both are recalled).
4. **The "No" campaign's central claim is factually wrong for every recall that has ever happened.** "Politicians choose your replacement" applies only to non-gubernatorial constitutional officers, which have been recalled **zero times** since 1913. For the 9 legislative recalls → voters choose in a special election. For the Governor → voters' own elected Lt. Governor, or a majority-vote election.
5. **It's the norm, not an outlier.** Of 19 recall states, only California and Colorado put both questions on one ballot (NCSL). And California already adopted this exact model for **local** recalls in 2022 (AB 2582) — uncontroversially.
6. **Cost is a wash.** LAO: net effect unknown, ±millions, under 0.1% of the General Fund. The 2021 recall cost $200.2M; dropping a 46–135 name replacement ballot saves real money.
7. **The cited flaw is overstated.** The SF Chronicle's deal-breaker (a recalled official may run again) is the Little Hoover Commission's *own* recommended design and the practice in AZ, NV, ND and WI. And someone just fired by a majority is unlikely to win a majority runoff.
8. Nonpartisan backing: League of Women Voters, California Common Cause, Secretary of State Weber, and Dan Schnur (former Republican FPPC chair).

**What I weighed against it:** It's plainly partisan in origin — written by a recalled Democrat, passed nearly on party lines — and in the last two years of a gubernatorial term it lets a recall produce *zero* change of direction. The Little Hoover Commission preferred a "snap election" and wanted the 12%-of-votes-cast signature threshold raised; Prop 5 does neither. Those are legitimate. But the removal power is untouched, the rules are facially neutral (they'd equally protect a Republican governor from a plurality coup), and the defect being fixed is concrete, documented, and anti-majoritarian.
