Choosing how to vote on California Proposition 3 depends on your priorities regarding state taxation, public education and healthcare funding, and government spending accountability, so it cannot be answered with a simple "yes" or "no." Below is a summary of what the measure does and the main arguments on both sides to help you make your own decision.

---

### What Proposition 3 Does
* **Background:** In 2012, voters approved Proposition 30 to temporarily raise personal income tax rates on the top ~2% of California earners. In 2016, voters approved Proposition 55 to extend those rates through 2030.
* **The Proposal:** Proposition 3 amends the California Constitution to make those higher marginal income tax rates (10.3%, 11.3%, and 12.3%) **permanent** instead of letting them expire in 2031 (when the rate for those brackets would otherwise revert to 9.3%).
* **Who Pays:** The rates apply to taxable income over approximately $371,000 for single filers, $742,000 for joint filers, and $505,000 for heads of household (2025 levels, adjusted annually for inflation).
* **Fiscal Impact:** According to the Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO), the measure would maintain between **$5 billion and $15 billion** in annual state revenue after 2030, depending on economic and stock market conditions. Roughly 40% of the overall fiscal benefit goes toward K–12 schools (89% of the Education Protection Account) and community colleges (11%), while the rest increases General Fund availability for programs like Medi-Cal, budget reserves, and other state services.

---

### Arguments for Voting YES
* **Prevents Cuts to Schools and Healthcare:** Supporters argue that letting the tax expire would create a $5 billion to $15 billion annual hole in the state budget, leading to teacher layoffs, larger class sizes, and reductions in Medi-Cal and children's healthcare coverage.
* **Maintains Existing Rates Without Taxing 98% of Californians:** Proponents emphasize that Proposition 3 does not raise taxes above current levels; it keeps in place rates that the wealthiest 2% of earners have already been paying since 2012.
* **Constitutional Safeguards and Audits:** Education Protection Account funds go directly to local school districts and community colleges, cannot be spent on administrative salaries or state bureaucracy, and are subject to annual independent audits and public online accounting.
* **Notable Supporters:** California Teachers Association, California Federation of Teachers, California State PTA, California School Nurses Organization, Planned Parenthood Affiliates of California, and California Professional Firefighters.

---

### Arguments for Voting NO
* **Holds the State to Its "Temporary" Promise:** Opponents point out that when the tax was passed in 2012 and extended in 2016, it was explicitly pitched to voters as a temporary measure to address budget challenges. Making it permanent, they argue, breaks that commitment to taxpayers.
* **Focuses on State Spending Rather Than Revenue:** Critics contend that California has a spending problem rather than a revenue problem—citing rapid growth in state spending over the past decade alongside recent budget deficits and high-profile losses to waste or fraud—and argue lawmakers should control spending before locking in permanent taxes.
* **High Tax Burden and Cost of Living:** Opponents note that Californians already face the nation's highest top marginal income tax rates, sales taxes, gas taxes, and overall cost of living, and argue that permanently locking in high tax rates hurts small/family businesses and economic competitiveness.
* **Notable Opponents:** California Taxpayers Association, Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, Family Business Association of California, and California Hispanic Chambers of Commerce.

---

### How to Weigh Your Vote
* Vote **YES** if you believe the higher income tax rates on the state's top 2% of earners should become a permanent source of revenue to support public schools, community colleges, Medi-Cal, and state budget reserves.
* Vote **NO** if you believe the tax increase should expire in 2031 as currently scheduled, either to reduce the state's top income tax rates or to pressure the state government to manage within existing baseline revenues.
