Whether it is better to vote **yes** or **no** on California Proposition 4 cannot be answered with a single word, as the choice depends on your personal priorities regarding campaign finance reform, local government flexibility, and the use of public taxpayer funds. 

### Overview of Proposition 4
Placed on the ballot by the Legislature (SB 42), Proposition 4—the **California Fair Elections Act of 2026**—would repeal California's existing ban on the use of public funds for political candidates' campaigns. 
* **What it does:** It does not directly establish a public campaign financing program, but instead allows the state and local governments to create such programs in the future if they choose. 
* **Rules and restrictions:** Any future program could not use money earmarked for education, transportation, or public safety. Participating candidates would have to demonstrate broad-based support in their district (such as collecting small-dollar contributions or vouchers) and agree to voluntary expenditure limits. Public funds could not be used to pay legal defense fees, fines, or personal campaign loans. The measure also triples the maximum fine for illegal campaign contributions or expenditures by foreign entities.
* **Fiscal impact:** According to the Legislative Analyst's Office, the measure would result in minor ongoing state costs of a few hundred thousand dollars per year for the Fair Political Practices Commission (FPPC) to provide guidance. Any larger future costs to the state or local governments would depend entirely on whether they choose to create public financing programs and how those programs are structured.

### Reasons to Vote **YES**
A **Yes** vote means state and local governments would be permitted to establish public campaign financing programs for candidates running for public office.

* **Reducing the Influence of Wealthy Donors:** Supporters (including the League of Women Voters of California, California Common Cause, Consumer Watchdog, and the California Nurses Association) argue that public financing options—such as small-dollar donation matching—help counter the influence of wealthy donors and special interests in elections.
* **Lowering Barriers for Candidates:** Proponents contend that allowing public financing makes elections more competitive by enabling qualified candidates from all walks of life to run for office without needing personal wealth or access to affluent donor networks.
* **Local Choice with Safeguards:** Supporters emphasize that California is currently the only state with a statewide ban on public campaign financing for non-charter jurisdictions, and that Proposition 4 simply gives voters and local governments the option to adopt programs while protecting funds earmarked for education, transportation, and public safety.

---

### Reasons to Vote **NO**
A **No** vote means the state and most local governments (except certain charter cities) would remain prohibited from creating public campaign financing programs for candidates.

* **Protecting Taxpayer Funds:** Opponents (including the California Taxpayers Association, the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, and several former FPPC commissioners) argue that public tax dollars should go toward public services rather than funding politicians' campaigns, negative ads, and campaign mailers.
* **Lack of Specific Caps and Rules:** Critics argue that Proposition 4 is an open-ended "blank check" because it sets no overarching cap on the total amount of public money a candidate can receive or the total number of candidates who can qualify, leaving the specific criteria and spending limits to be written by elected officials themselves.
* **Does Not Eliminate Private or Special Interest Money:** Opponents point out that Proposition 4 does not ban private or special-interest contributions, meaning participating candidates could still raise private donations alongside receiving taxpayer funds.

---

### Summary
* **Vote YES** if you believe state and local governments should have the option to create publicly funded campaign programs to reduce candidates' reliance on wealthy donors, subject to the measure's earmark and spending restrictions.
* **Vote NO** if you believe taxpayer money should remain prohibited from funding political campaigns or think the measure leaves too much discretion to lawmakers to set funding rules and limits.
