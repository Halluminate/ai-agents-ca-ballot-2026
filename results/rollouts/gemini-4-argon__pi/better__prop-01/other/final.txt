Whether it is better to vote **yes** or **no** on California Proposition 1 cannot be answered with a simple "yes" or "no," as the choice depends on how you weigh the state's need for subsidized affordable housing against the long-term fiscal impact of taking on additional state bond debt.

Here is an overview of the measure and the primary considerations on each side based on the Official Voter Information Guide:

### Overview of Proposition 1
Proposition 1 authorizes **$11.25 billion** in state general obligation bonds for housing affordability programs:
* **$10 billion** for state housing programs, repaid from the state's General Fund:
  * **$7.2 billion** for acquiring, building, renovating, or preserving affordable multifamily rental and supportive housing.
  * **$1.1 billion** for homeownership and downpayment assistance for low- and moderate-income first-time homebuyers.
  * **$500 million** for housing-related infrastructure (such as water, sewage, parks, and transportation).
  * **$450 million** for farmworker housing loans and grants.
  * **$350 million** for affordable student housing at UC and CSU campuses.
  * **$200 million** for tribal housing grants.
  * **$200 million** for local pilot programs aimed at reducing housing development costs.
* **$1.25 billion** for home loan assistance for California veterans (CalVet Home Loan Program), repaid directly by participating veterans through their monthly mortgage payments at no direct cost to the state.

---

### Reasons to Consider Voting YES
* **Addresses Housing Affordability and Homelessness:** Supporters—including veterans' groups, labor unions, senior advocates, and housing organizations—emphasize that California faces a severe housing shortage. The Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) estimates the bond would subsidize up to 40,000 multifamily rental units, 2,500 farmworker units, and 1,200 university student beds, while assisting up to 40,000 households with homeownership.
* **Supports Veterans and Vulnerable Populations:** The measure dedicates $1.25 billion to help roughly 2,100 veterans purchase homes and targets rental and supportive housing toward low-income seniors, people with disabilities, essential workers, and individuals at risk of homelessness.
* **Leverages Federal Funds Without Raising Taxes:** Repayment comes from the existing General Fund and veterans' loan payments rather than new tax increases, and state bond funding helps developments qualify for federal matching funds and tax credits.

---

### Reasons to Consider Voting NO
*(Note: No formal argument against Proposition 1 was submitted for the Official Voter Information Guide, and the measure passed the Legislature by votes of 29–2 in the Senate and 61–7 in the Assembly. However, standard fiscal and policy trade-offs noted in the Legislative Analyst's analysis include the following:)*
* **Long-Term State Debt and Interest Costs:** Repaying the $10 billion portion of the bond will cost the state's General Fund an estimated **$500 million to $600 million annually for about 25 years** (roughly 0.25% of the total General Fund budget). Because the state must pay interest to bondholders, borrowing the money costs about 15% more (after adjusting for inflation) than paying for programs up front.
* **Opportunity Cost in the State Budget:** Because General Fund revenues are used to pay for core public services—such as K–12 education, higher education, and health care—committing $500 million to $600 million per year to bond debt service reduces the funds available for other state priorities or future budget shortfalls.
* **Scale and Approach to the Housing Crisis:** Critics of state housing bonds often argue that government subsidies only reach a small fraction of California's overall housing market (where roughly 100,000 units are built mostly with private capital each year) and that reducing regulatory barriers, construction costs, and zoning restrictions is a more cost-effective way to lower housing prices than taking on new public debt.

---

### Summary
* Vote **Yes** if you believe the state should borrow $11.25 billion to expand affordable rental housing, supportive housing, and homeownership assistance for low-income residents and veterans, and you accept the 25-year General Fund repayment cost.
* Vote **No** if you oppose increasing the state's long-term bond debt and interest obligations, prefer to keep General Fund revenues available for other budget priorities, or favor non-borrowing approaches to addressing housing costs.
