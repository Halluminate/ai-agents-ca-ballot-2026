Whether it is better to vote "yes" or "no" on California Proposition 42 is a value judgment that depends on your priorities regarding tax policy, state revenue flexibility, and competing measures on the November 2026 ballot. Because there is no objective consensus, I cannot answer with a simple "yes" or "no," but here is a breakdown of the measure and the arguments on both sides to help you decide.

### What Proposition 42 Does
Proposition 42 is a constitutional amendment that would:
* **Prohibit new state personal property taxes:** Bar the state from creating new taxes on the ownership of personal property (everything people own other than real estate), including financial assets, stocks, investment accounts, retirement accounts (such as 401(k)s, IRAs, and pensions), intellectual property, and business interests.
* **Restrict retroactive state taxes:** Prohibit new state taxes that apply retroactively to past conduct, income, or residency status prior to the tax's effective date (with limited exceptions for declared emergencies).
* **Nullify conflicting measures:** Void any conflicting measure on the same ballot enacted on or after January 1, 2026—most notably **Proposition 40** (a proposed one-time 5% wealth tax on individuals with assets over $1 billion)—if Proposition 42 receives more "yes" votes.

According to the Legislative Analyst's Office, the measure would restrict the state's options to raise taxes in the future, which could result in lower future state tax revenues than would otherwise occur.

---

### Arguments for Voting YES
Supporters—including the California Professional Firefighters, the State Building and Construction Trades Council of California, the California Small Business Association, and AMVETS Department of California—argue that a **YES** vote:
* **Protects retirement and personal savings:** Ensures the state cannot impose taxes on the value of unrealized holdings in 401(k)s, IRAs, pensions, and savings accounts.
* **Prevents "double taxation":** Stops the state from taxing money a second time simply for being held in savings or investments after residents have already paid income tax when earning it.
* **Provides tax predictability:** Bars retroactive tax increases, ensuring that individuals, retirees, and businesses can plan their finances under stable rules that cannot be changed after the fact.

---

### Arguments for Voting NO
Opponents—including SEIU-United Healthcare Workers West and U.S. Senator Bernie Sanders—argue that a **NO** vote:
* **Preserves the California Billionaire Tax (Proposition 40):** Contends that Proposition 42 was funded by billionaires specifically to invalidate Proposition 40, which would levy a one-time 5% tax on billionaire wealth primarily to fund healthcare programs.
* **Maintains state revenue options:** Keeps open the state's ability to raise revenue from concentrated wealth and financial assets in the future rather than locking permanent restrictions into the state Constitution.
* **Avoids unnecessary constitutional limits:** Argues that ordinary retirement accounts are not the target of current wealth tax proposals, and that the measure uses broad language about retirement savings to shield extreme wealth from taxation.

---

### Summary
* **Vote YES** if you want to constitutionally prohibit the state from imposing new taxes on the ownership of financial assets, personal property, and retirement accounts, or from enacting retroactive taxes—including blocking the proposed billionaire wealth tax (Proposition 40).
* **Vote NO** if you want the state to retain the authority to tax financial assets or personal property—or if you support Proposition 40's one-time wealth tax on billionaires to fund healthcare and do not want Proposition 42 to nullify it.
