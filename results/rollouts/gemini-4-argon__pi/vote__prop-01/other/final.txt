Whether to vote **yes** or **no** on California’s Proposition 1 (November 2026 ballot) depends on how you weigh the need for state-funded affordable housing against the long-term fiscal cost of taking on additional state bond debt. Because that choice comes down to your personal priorities, I can’t give a single yes-or-no recommendation, but here is a breakdown of the measure and the arguments on both sides to help you decide.

---

### What Proposition 1 Does
Placed on the ballot by the Legislature (SB 417), Proposition 1 authorizes **$11.25 billion** in state general obligation bonds for housing programs:
* **$7.2 billion** to build, acquire, or renovate affordable multifamily rental housing and supportive housing for people experiencing homelessness ($5.1 billion for new construction/rehabilitation and $2.1 billion for preservation/supportive housing).
* **$1.25 billion** for the CalVet Home Loan Program to provide mortgages to California veterans.
* **$1.1 billion** for homeownership and downpayment assistance for low- and moderate-income first-time homebuyers.
* **$500 million** for housing-related infrastructure (such as water, sewer, parks, and transportation).
* **$450 million** for farmworker housing, **$350 million** for UC and CSU student housing, and **$200 million** for tribal housing grants.
* **$200 million** for local pilot programs aimed at lowering housing development costs.

### Fiscal Impact
According to the Legislative Analyst’s Office (LAO):
* **General Fund Cost:** Repaying the $10 billion portion for state housing programs would cost the state’s General Fund an estimated **$500 million to $600 million per year for about 25 years** (about 0.25% of the total General Fund budget). Because of interest payments, borrowing costs roughly 15% more (after adjusting for inflation) than paying for projects up front.
* **Veterans Program Cost:** The $1.25 billion veterans’ mortgage portion is repaid directly by participating veterans through their monthly mortgage payments, resulting in no direct cost to the state.
* **Projected Output:** The LAO estimates the bond would help fund up to 40,000 multifamily rental units, 2,500 farmworker units, and 1,200 student beds, while assisting up to 40,000 households (including about 2,100 veterans) with buying or repairing a home.

---

### Reasons to Vote YES
**A YES vote authorizes the state to borrow $11.25 billion for affordable housing and veterans' home loans.**
* **Addresses the housing and homelessness crisis:** Supporters—including Habitat for Humanity California, US Vets, the California Federation of Teachers, and Self-Help for the Elderly—argue that public investment is urgently needed to jump-start stalled, "shovel-ready" affordable housing projects and keep vulnerable residents (seniors, people with disabilities, and low-income families) from becoming homeless.
* **Supports essential workers and first-time buyers:** Provides downpayment assistance and affordable units so teachers, nurses, firefighters, and working families can afford to live in the communities where they work.
* **Helps veterans buy homes:** Replenishes funding for the CalVet Home Loan Program at no direct cost to taxpayers.
* **Unlocks federal matching funds without raising taxes:** State bond dollars help developers qualify for federal low-income housing tax credits, bringing federal funds back to California without creating new state taxes.

### Reasons to Vote NO
**A NO vote prevents the state from borrowing $11.25 billion for these housing programs.**
* **Increases state debt and General Fund obligations:** While no formal opposition argument was submitted to the Official Voter Information Guide, legislators and editorial boards opposing the measure argue that California should not add $10 billion in taxpayer-backed debt—committing $500 million to $600 million annually for 25 years—while facing structural budget deficits.
* **Does not fix the root causes of high housing costs:** Critics contend that subsidizing construction with borrowed money fails to address the underlying regulatory, permitting, and environmental rules that make building housing in California so expensive in the first place.
* **Higher cost due to interest:** Paying for ongoing housing needs through long-term bonds increases total costs to taxpayers by roughly 15% after inflation due to interest payments, reducing future General Fund flexibility for other priorities like education or healthcare.
* **Small share dedicated to veterans:** Some opponents criticize the measure’s framing around veterans, noting that only about 11% ($1.25 billion of the $11.25 billion total) goes specifically toward the veterans' home loan program.

---

### Bottom Line
* Vote **YES** if you believe the state should borrow money now to expand affordable rental housing, homelessness prevention, and homeownership assistance for low-income residents and veterans, and you accept the $500–$600 million annual General Fund repayment cost.
* Vote **NO** if you believe the state should avoid taking on new long-term debt, that housing programs should be funded through the regular budget rather than bonds, or that California should focus on regulatory reform rather than subsidies to lower housing costs.
