## Research summary: California Proposition 44 (Nov. 3, 2026)

**What it does:** Requires nonprofit Federally Qualified Health Centers (and "look-alikes") to spend ≥90% of **total revenue** on "program services," or pay the state a penalty equal to the shortfall. Penalties are escrowed 5 years and refundable if the clinic comes into compliance. The Attorney General defines what counts via **binding guidance that can be issued by bulletin, bypassing normal rulemaking**. CDPH can grant one-year waivers. Adds a private right of action letting any patient sue a noncompliant clinic, plus annual fees on clinics to fund enforcement.

**The structural flaw that decides this:** The denominator is *revenue*, not expenses. A clinic that runs a prudent surplus or builds reserves is mechanically pushed below 90% and fined in cash **within 30 days** — even if every dollar it spends is well spent. It effectively caps overhead + surplus + reserves *combined* at 10% of revenue. Clinics currently average ~80% (LAO). The waiver valve requires proving **going-concern doubt under GAAP** — you must be near collapse to get relief, which is backwards.

**Who it falls on:** Only nonprofit FQHCs — the safety net serving 7M+ Californians and ~1 in 3 Medi-Cal patients — not hospitals, for-profits, or medical groups. And it lands while federal H.R. 1 Medicaid cuts are already squeezing these same clinics.

**Corroboration and caveats:** The Berkeley Research Group estimate (91% of 202 clinic organizations fail; ~$1.7B year-one penalties; 88% negative margins) was commissioned by the opposing California Primary Care Association, and "$1.7B cut" overstates it since penalties are technically recoverable. But the LAO independently confirms the average is ~80%, confirms enforcement costs are paid *by fees on the clinics themselves*, and calls other effects "uncertain" including possible clinic closures.

**Process signal:** The Legislature already considered nearly the same thing (AB 1113) and it **died in committee**. FQHCs are already heavily regulated — HRSA Section 330 rules, mandatory public audits, public Form 990s, state law requiring board review of CEO/CFO pay, and boards that must be majority patients.

**Motive:** Sole sponsor and funder is SEIU-UHW. The union spent just **~$20,000** on the campaign July 1–Sept 19 while opponents spent $32M+ — consistent with the pending federal RICO suit alleging the union offered to kill Prop 44 in exchange for unionization concessions at 25,000 clinic workers. The union denies this, and an earlier suit to remove the measure was dismissed. Either way, this fits SEIU-UHW's documented leverage playbook (three failed dialysis props, ~$340M spent; the 2023 $25/hr health wage won via initiative threat).

**The consensus is unusually one-sided:** Governor Newsom — no. **All three** major parties (Democratic, Republican, Working Families) — no. CMA, AAP-California, AAFP, ACOG District IX, ACEP-California, CA School Nurses Organization, Planned Parenthood Affiliates, CA Hospital Association, CA Teachers Association, CA Chamber of Commerce, Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, NASW-California, 300+ organizations and 1,000+ clinics — no. **Zero media endorsements in support**; every editorial board that weighed in (LA Times, SF Chronicle, Sacramento Bee/McClatchy, San Diego Union-Tribune, OC Register, East Bay Times, Press Democrat, Santa Cruz Sentinel) says no. Money: $16.9M vs. $38.6M. PPIC: 34/61 in September, 25/71 in October — the least popular measure on the ballot, with no partisan split.

**My reasoning:** The underlying concern is real and not a pretext — some clinics do report 30–40% administrative costs and CEO pay up to $1.9M while frontline staffing lags, and published mission-spend ratios are a genuinely good idea. But "spend 90% on patient care" is a slogan that the actual text does not implement. It punishes reserves rather than waste, hands an elected partisan official unilateral power to redefine what counts as care, exempts itself from normal rulemaking, and imposes it exclusively on the providers serving California's poorest patients at the worst possible moment. Transparency and executive-compensation disclosure could be achieved by statute, board reform, or the AG's existing charitable-trust authority without draining liquidity from the safety net. A badly drafted mandate that risks closing rural and school-based clinics is a worse outcome than the over-spending it targets.

**No**
