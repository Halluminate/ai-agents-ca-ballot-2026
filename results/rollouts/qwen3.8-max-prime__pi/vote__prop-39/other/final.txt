## My answer: **NO**

I read the actual constitutional text, the Legislative Analyst's fiscal estimate, the Attorney General's title and summary, the ballot arguments, both court rulings, the campaign finance filings, the Secretary of State's ballot-rejection data, and the editorial positions across the state. Here's why I land on no.

---

### What's actually in the text (Art. II, §3.1)

- In-person: show government-issued ID **every time**, in "any election in the State."
- By mail: handwrite the last four digits of a designated government ID number on the envelope. Officials "shall only count" the ballot after verifying identity.
- Officials must use "best efforts" to verify citizenship using "government data" — **both terms undefined**.
- Free state voter ID card on request; State Auditor compliance audits in odd years; **any citizen may sue** the state or any county over compliance; declared "self-executing."

### The five findings that decided this for me

**1. The main benefit being advertised is not in the measure.** DeMaio and the Yes campaign say the four-digit PIN *replaces* signature matching. It doesn't — Prop 39 never repeals Elections Code §3019. The Secretary of State's own guide says a yes vote means voters provide "**additional** identifying information." Common Cause, Loyola election-law scholars, former Maricopa County recorder Stephen Richer (R), Assembly Elections Chair Gail Pellerin (a former registrar), and Verified Voting all agree: mail voters get **two** independent ways to be rejected instead of one. Even *Reason*, which is sympathetic to voter ID, says it would "no doubt burden our excruciatingly slow ballot counting."

**2. There is no cure process — for a brand-new failure point.** Today, a signature problem triggers a strong statutory safety net: a presumption your signature is valid, "an exact match is not required," rejection only if two extra officials each find a mismatch "beyond a reasonable doubt," notice by mail plus call/text/email, and ~22 days to fix it. Prop 39 has **no cure provision at all**. The AG's official summary says the ballot is simply "invalidated" — write your driver's license number when you designated your SSN and your vote doesn't count. Yet on Nov. 2 the Yes campaign told the East Bay Times it "allows a 'curring' process if a voter transposes numbers." That promise exists only in a press quote, not in the constitution they're asking you to ratify. Texas is the cautionary case: after its 2021 mail-ballot ID law, rejections went from under 1% to **over 12%** — roughly 1 in 8 votes invalidated — and a peer-reviewed study found turnout fell.

**3. The pro argument's central statistic is false, and a judge already ruled against this campaign twice.** The ballot argument claims "more than 100,000 ballots were rejected due to signature mismatches" in the last election. SOS data: **71,387** statewide in Nov. 2024 (43,976 in June 2026). Separately, Judge Shelleyanne Chang found the proponents' claims that Prop 39 makes voting "easier," protects privacy, and "saves millions" to be **false and misleading** — writing that the measure actually makes ID numbers "less private, and less secure," and that savings claims were "objectively untrue." They were forced to rewrite their argument. Judge Jennifer Rockwell then upheld the AG's title — "**Prohibits** citizens from voting unless they present government-issued identification" — rejecting the campaign's own insistence that it bars nobody from voting, because "vote" plainly includes the counting. When a measure's sponsors lose both of their own lawsuits over how it's described, that's information.

**4. The citizenship-verification mandate can't be executed.** There is no comprehensive government list of U.S. citizens. Federal databases track the immigration status of the foreign-born — not the birthright citizenship of the ~90% of Americans born here. That's why E-Verify has been resisted for decades and why courts have repeatedly blocked comparable mandates. The risk runs at eligible citizens being wrongly flagged and purged. Notably, the **San Diego Union-Tribune** — in DeMaio's own region, and a board that says "what's the big deal?" about in-person ID in 36 other states — still recommends no on this ground alone: "the records needed to do it accurately don't exist."

**5. The problem is tiny; the exposure is enormous.** The California State Library reviewed grand jury reports from 14 counties covering 2020–2024 and found **no evidence of systemic or significant fraud**. The Heritage Foundation — the conservative think tank behind Project 2025 — documents just **71** California voter-fraud cases across 1982–2025, all prosecuted. Brookings: "no election outcome in the US has ever been altered by ballot fraud." Against that: 13 million mail ballots per presidential cycle and roughly **22 million existing registrants** who would each have to affirmatively re-designate an ID (the LAO confirms this applies to "new and existing voters"; the Budget & Policy Center says it "effectively means" re-registering) in a state where 80%+ vote by mail. A 2026 University of Maryland analysis found ~2.7 million Californians (11% of eligible voters) have no driver's license, another 13% hold one that's expired or has a stale address or name, and 3+ million would struggle to produce citizenship documents — with people of color, young, disabled, low-income, and married women who changed names overrepresented. And you'd be writing the last four of a government ID — possibly an SSN — on the *outside* of an envelope next to your name, address, and signature, when Gov. Code §7924.000 currently keeps those numbers strictly confidential.

### The strongest case for yes, and why it falls short

It's real and I don't want to strawman it. Voter ID is popular in the abstract; 36 states have some form, as do Canada and Mexico. California verifies 80%+ of voters by handwriting judgment applied unevenly across 58 counties. Our rolls do carry stale records, ballots do get mailed to people who've moved, and ballot harvesting has genuine abuse potential. The ID is free, mail voting and extended hours survive, and a former Republican Secretary of State, Bill Jones, supports it. Aggregate research (a 2019 NBER review; *Research & Politics*, Dec. 2024) finds strict ID laws have minimal net turnout effects.

But none of that rescues **this drafting**. Add a private right of action letting anyone sue any of 58 counties over undefined compliance duties and you get what Loyola's Justin Levitt called a "generic litigation trap" and the Chronicle called "CEQA for voting rights" — permanent litigation cloud over every result. Put it in the constitution as self-executing with the operative terms blank, and the errors are much harder to fix than a statute's: correcting them later takes another statewide vote or a two-thirds legislative amendment that "furthers the purposes of the act." The LAO prices it at tens of millions to low hundreds of millions **every year**. And the "bipartisan" framing doesn't survive the finance reports: Richard Uihlein of Wisconsin gave $17M (51.6%) and Joseph Gebbia $8M (24.3%) — two donors, ~76% of the money — with **80.3% of Yes funds from outside California**, and every major political endorser a Republican. PPIC found 85% of Republicans and 19% of Democrats in favor. The measure polled competitively in September, then fell to 37–41% yes in the final PPIC, Berkeley IGS, and Citrin/Politico surveys as voters learned the details — which is what you'd expect when the specifics are worse than the slogan.

Finally, the trust rationale is undercut by the proponents' own data: PPIC shows confidence in how California counts votes **rose** from 47% to 63% among likely voters between 2017 and 2024. And multiple studies find strict ID laws barely move voter confidence, because confidence tracks partisanship and whether your candidate won. When the Mercury News board repeatedly asked the Yes campaign for evidence of fraud, they report the campaign "deflected, dodged and denied the premise."

Editorial boards: **No** — SF Chronicle, Sacramento Bee/McClatchy, San Diego Union-Tribune, Mercury News/East Bay Times, Santa Cruz Sentinel. **Yes** — OC Register, LA Daily News. Opposed by the CMA, ACLU, Common Cause, League of Women Voters, and the UCLA Voting Rights Project.

**Vote no.** If you want election-integrity reform, the durable route is statutory: fund county election offices, let counties join the multistate voter-roll accuracy consortium they've been requesting, tighten who may collect ballots, and require counting to begin the day ballots are mailed. That addresses the legitimate concerns without entrenching an undefined, cure-less mandate in the state constitution.
