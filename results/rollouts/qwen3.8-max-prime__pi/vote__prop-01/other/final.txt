## **YES**

**Prop 1 (Nov 3, 2026) — $11.25B housing bond. Vote YES.**

Why, based on the research:

**The value is unusually good.** The LAO projects up to **40,000 affordable rental units**, ~2,500 farmworker units, 1,200 student beds, plus preservation of 5,500+ existing affordable homes and homeownership help for up to 40,000 households — for roughly **$250K of state subsidy per unit**. Enterprise Community Partners found ~$3.60–$4 of federal/local/private money leveraged per state dollar.

**The money targets a documented, shovel-ready backlog.** ~39,880 units across 461 developments are fully designed, entitled and partially financed, stalled purely on a funding gap of about **$4.1B**. The 2018 bond was exhausted by 2023; the Multifamily Housing Program is oversubscribed ~9-to-1; the state allocated only ~$1.8B this year with no ongoing funding. This is the highest-leverage marginal dollar available, not speculative spending.

**The fiscal cost is small and the debt structure is appropriate.** $500–600M/year for ~25 years is **0.25% of the General Fund**. California's debt-service ratio is ~3% of GF revenues, *below* its ~4% historical average — even with Prop 1 and Prop 38 added. Critically, funded projects carry **55-year affordability covenants**, so the asset's useful life far exceeds the 25–30 year repayment. That is exactly what general obligation bonds are for; paying cash would shift the cost onto the low-income Californians who benefit decades out.

**The opposition is thin.** No argument against was submitted, **zero dollars** reported to any "No" committee, and the state Republican Party is officially *neutral*. Opposition is limited to a handful of legislators and some editorial boards.

**The best counterargument, and why it doesn't flip the vote:** LAO projects an ~$18B problem in 2026-27 growing to ~$35B annually, and debt service is legally fixed while program spending can be cut — so this does reduce flexibility (Skelton, Mercury News, U-T, Press Democrat, Howard Jarvis all make this case). But $580M isn't what's driving a structural deficit rooted in health care, education and pensions; by that logic California could never finance *any* long-lived capital asset. The "doesn't fix root causes" critique is also weak — the Legislature has already passed major streamlining (and Prop 45 addresses permitting on this same ballot). Funding and reform are complements. The genuine weaknesses — the somewhat misleading "Veterans" branding (11% of funds, and vets repay that part themselves) and ~$1.1B in down-payment assistance that could bid up prices — are real but small relative to the whole.

Bottom line: modest, below-historical-average debt cost against 40,000 permanently affordable homes that are already permitted and waiting. **Yes.**
