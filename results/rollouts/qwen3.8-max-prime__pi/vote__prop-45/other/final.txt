## My answer: **NO**

### What Prop 45 is (Nov 3, 2026 California ballot)
An initiative statute sponsored by the California Chamber of Commerce that creates an optional "streamlined" track under CEQA for "essential projects": housing, water, clean energy, public health, public safety/wildfire, broadband, schools, and transportation. It sets shot clocks (365 business days for an EIR; 270 days for courts), requires agencies to state application requirements up front, lets the applicant supply the single project alternative, judges impacts by laws in effect at application, caps public comment, narrows judicial review, and restricts tribal consultation to federally recognized tribes. LAO estimates initial state/local costs in the high tens of millions, potentially >$100M/year. Polling (PPIC, Oct 16–22): 46% yes / 50% no.

### Why the "yes" case is real but not enough
CEQA abuse is genuine — litigation adds ~2 years on average (Little Hoover Commission, 2024), and permitting delays add real cost to housing. Enforcing deadlines and cutting redundant paperwork is, as even the *SF Chronicle* editorial board put it, "a no-brainer." Projects remain subject to the Clean Air/Water Acts, Coastal Act, ESA, and local approval authority, and skilled-workforce/prevailing-wage standards largely carry over (PRC 21183.5). 28 editorial boards plus the *Wall Street Journal*, the Sacramento Bee/McClatchy board, Mercury News/East Bay Times, and San Diego Union-Tribune said yes.

### Why I still vote no
1. **The housing payoff is mostly already banked.** AB 130 (2025) already exempted most infill housing from CEQA. Prop 45's marginal reach is sprawl/greenfield subdivisions, freeway expansions, carbon capture/hydrogen, and "all related and ancillary" infrastructure — the project types that increase VMT, emissions, and wildfire exposure.
2. **It moves decision power from agencies and the public to applicants** (§21022 vesting into thresholds; §21025 applicant-defined single alternative, which may be a mere component of the project itself).
3. **It shrinks public input**: hard caps of 20/45 days with no extension, and late comments excluded from the record entirely (§21021).
4. **It strips tribal rights**: AB 52 consultation is replaced and limited to federally recognized tribes (§21028(e)), sidelining dozens of California tribes (Kizh/Gabrieleño, Tataviam, Amah Mutsun, Ramaytush Ohlone). A federally recognized tribe is among the largest yes donors ($750K).
5. **It guts judicial remedies**: review limited to "objective existing laws"; a court may *never* rescind an approval; relief is confined to the noncomplying part/phase; injunctions require clear and convincing evidence of a *public safety* harm; and CEQA compliance is judged "solely" under an interpretive clause directing courts to give "the fullest possible weight to the… approval and realization of essential projects" (§§21022, 21026–21029).
6. **It's a one-way ratchet**: all cross-referenced statutes are frozen as they existed on **December 31, 2025** (§21031), the state "occupies the field" (§21030), amendment requires two-thirds *and* must further the act's purposes (§21034), and it creates a permanent General Fund appropriation for private counsel to defend the act if the Governor/AG won't (§4).
7. **Unresolved scope = the litigation it promises to end.** Data centers aren't excluded even though high-speed rail, Delta conveyance, nuclear, jails, and new oil/gas are; the "broadband… incidental to and enable" definition splits legal experts (Biber/Frank/McKerley vs. Thomason/Chong), and yes-campaign committees took ~$1M from data-center developers in the final week.
8. **The affordability promise is unenforceable** — nothing requires savings to be passed through to buyers, renters, or ratepayers.
9. **Credible reformers aren't buying it**: Scott Wiener, California YIMBY, SPUR, Tom Steyer, Darrell Steinberg, and rural-county advocates are neutral or opposed; Berkeley's Ethan Elkind calls it "a sea change," not surgery; the League of Women Voters, Sierra Club California, the American Lung Association, and the state Democratic and Republican *county* parties (six of them) split accordingly.

A no vote doesn't freeze reform — the Legislature has been streamlining CEQA by majority vote for a decade and can enact shot clocks without permanently disabling itself. A yes vote installs a 41-page, near-unamendable rewrite that trades away public, tribal, and judicial checks for speed, with the promised affordability left to faith.
