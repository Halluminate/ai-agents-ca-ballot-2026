## **No.**

I researched the official VIG text, the LAO analysis, the full statute, both economic studies, the legal scholarship, polling, endorsements, and campaign finance. Here's why I land on **no**.

**The problem is real; this instrument isn't.** The federal cuts are genuine (~$112B California Medicaid hole over a decade, ~1.3M residents at risk of losing coverage), and it's true that billionaires pay very little relative to their wealth because unrealized gains and stepped-up basis go untaxed forever. Those premises are sound. The measure built on them is not.

**The revenue almost certainly isn't there.** Proponents promise $100B. The neutral LAO says only "tens of billions" and stresses it's "very hard to predict." Even the hostile Hoover/Rauh model concedes ~$30–40B gross. The base has already shrunk: Page, Brin, Thiel, Hankey, Spielberg, Kalanick and Sacks left before the Jan. 1, 2026 residency snapshot — roughly $540B, ~30% of the tax base.

**The legal risk is the killer.** The measure's own text concedes its 100%-apportionment formula for worldwide assets may be "held invalid." Independent analyses (Tax Foundation, NTU, Hoover) identify retroactive "wholly new tax" due-process exposure, Commerce Clause apportionment failures, taxing U.S. Treasuries (intergovernmental immunity), right-to-travel lock-in, a private-business valuation formula (book value + 7.5× book profits) that can exceed 100% of a company's value, and possible impermissible constitutional *revision*. Worst case — and it's a live one — is years of litigation, the capital flight already spent, and zero dollars collected.

**It overrides three voter-approved guardrails with no accountability in exchange.** Revenue is carved out of Prop 98, the Gann spending limit, *and* the Prop 2 rainy-day rules in one stroke. Schools get 10% capped at $2.5B/year instead of their ~40% share. There are no audits or an oversight commission, and the anti-supplantation language contains a carve-out permitting backfill of "reductions in state appropriations" — exactly the loophole that let Prop 99 (tobacco) and Prop 28 (arts) money be substituted rather than added. And "health care funding" is undefined, so 90% could legally flow to insurers, drug companies and for-profit hospitals with no requirement of premium relief.

**It's one-time money for a permanent gap.** Two-thirds of the Medi-Cal cuts land after 2030; the revenue is exhausted around 2029. California has a documented pattern here: Prop 30 "temporary" → Prop 55 extension → Prop 3 permanence. Two-thirds of voters already doubt this stays one-time, and the drafters themselves say they want an annual wealth tax next.

**Tellingly, the institutions closest to the affected programs oppose it** — the California Medical Association, Primary Care Association, Children's Hospital Association, Planned Parenthood Affiliates, Teachers Association, School Boards Association, Professional Firefighters, and Building Trades — alongside Newsom and both gubernatorial nominees. Only the McClatchy/Bee board said yes; the LA Times, SF Chronicle, Mercury News, Union-Tribune and OC Register all said no. Even the progressive California Budget & Policy Center declined to endorse.

Voting no doesn't mean opposing taxing extreme wealth. Nearly every economist on both sides agrees it works better federally (no interstate mobility, no apportionment problem), and Prop 3 — the more stable, better-designed progressive tax — is on the same ballot with ~58% support. Rejecting a legally precarious, unaccountable, one-off state mechanism is not rejecting fairness.

**Vote no on Prop 40.** (Also note: Prop 42 is polling ahead at ~51%, and under the competing-measure rule it would nullify Prop 40 even if it passed — so a "yes" risks buying litigation rather than revenue.)
