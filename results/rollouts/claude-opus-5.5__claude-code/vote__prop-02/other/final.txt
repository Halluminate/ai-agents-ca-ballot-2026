I'm not going to cast this vote for you. Prop 2 is a fight largely along party lines, and the research shows the facts aren't really in dispute: both sides agree it would **grow the state's reserves** and **make taxpayer refunds less likely**. Which of those matters more to you is the decision. Election Day is tomorrow, Nov. 3, and one question gets you to yes or no:

> **In the next boom, should extra tax money be saved, or should the state spending limit keep pushing it out as refunds and one-time projects?**
> - **Saved → YES**
> - **Refunded or spent, or you won't enlarge the fund until the rules for taking money out are tightened → NO**

### What it does (starts in 2027-28; this is not the 2024 school bond Prop 2)
- **Bigger cap:** The rainy day fund's limit doubles from 10% to 20% of state tax revenue, roughly $21B to $42B.
- **Bigger deposits in booms:** The state must save more when capital-gains tax revenue spikes.
- **Spending limit change:** Under the Gann limit (California's constitutional cap on state spending), savings would count when they're spent, not when they're deposited.
- **Debt payments:** Required extra debt payments are extended from 2030 to 2040. More debts would qualify, including school obligations, internal state loans and the ~$20B federal unemployment insurance loan.
- **Unchanged:** taxes, Prop 98 school funding and the rules for taking money out.
- **Small effect at first:** The fund holds $15.1B, below even today's cap, and the state is $27B under the spending limit. Prop 2 mostly matters in the next boom.

### Strongest case for YES
- California ranks about 5th among states for revenue swings but about 29th for reserves.
- The nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) estimates that in a bad scenario, current rules cover about a third of future shortfalls. This approach would cover about half.
- The LAO said the core of this plan "clearly improves upon current law." It had also recommended counting withdrawals against the limit, which Prop 2 does.
- In 2022, staying under the limit pushed about $48B of surplus into tax refunds (including a $9.5B one) and exempt one-time spending instead of savings. Former budget staffer Jason Sisney says leaders would have saved more without that pressure. About $82B in budget problems followed.

### Strongest case for NO
- **Fewer refunds is the point of the change, not a side effect.** A formal Gann refund has only been triggered once, in 1987, but the limit's pressure helped produce the 2022 refund.
- **Taking money out stays easy.** The LAO says the "budget emergency" test "can allow a fiscal emergency declaration during a sizeable budget surplus."
- **Recent behavior:** About $12B was withdrawn in 2024–26, and this year's budget skipped a $5.4B deposit. Critic David Crane, a former Schwarzenegger adviser, argues a bigger fund just lets governors "bake in spending."
- **Half a reform:** The LAO wanted the cap to reach 50% by 2055.
- **Unemployment loan:** Prop 2 would let saved money pay off the unemployment insurance loan, which employers would otherwise repay through federal payroll taxes. The progressive-leaning California Budget & Policy Center flagged this.

### Claims to discount
- **"Guarantees more spending" (No side):** The LAO's only fiscal finding is that reserves would be higher.
- **"Stops politicians from overspending" (Yes side):** Overstated. Nothing stops lawmakers from skipping deposits. Even the SF Chronicle endorsed it only "if our politicians bother to use it."

**Who's on each side:**
- **Yes:** Newsom, Democrats, Republican Assemblymember Josh Hoover, firefighters, hospitals, LA Area Chamber of Commerce, SPUR, SF Chronicle, Sacramento Bee.
- **No:** California Republican Party, Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, Reform California, Govern for California, San Diego Union-Tribune, Orange County Register.
