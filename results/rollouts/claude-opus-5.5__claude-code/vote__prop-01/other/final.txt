OpenAI gave the same amount on Oct. 13, so ChatGPT has the same conflict. You shouldn't take a voting call from an AI whose maker is funding one side.

The research also settled most of the factual questions. What's left is a value tradeoff, and that's yours to make. Here it is, set up so you can decide quickly. Election Day is tomorrow, Nov. 3.

**What it does:** It lets the state borrow $11.25 billion.
- **$10B** is repaid from the state budget at about **$500–600M a year for 25 years**. That's about 0.25% of the state's main budget (the General Fund).
- About **$7.2B** of that goes to subsidized rentals. The nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) estimates up to about 40,000 units. Another $1.1B goes to homebuyer help, and the rest to farmworker, student and tribal housing and infrastructure.
- **$1.25B** goes to veterans' home loans. Veterans repay these, so that part costs the state nothing.

**Vote YES if you think:**
- Subsidizing housing for low-income Californians is worth about 0.25% of the budget. These units must stay affordable for 55 years.
- Now is a good time to do it:
  - The 2018 housing bond money is used up, and the main program got only $200M in this year's budget.
  - About 40,000 approved affordable units are stalled for lack of funding, according to Enterprise Community Partners.
  - A 2025 federal tax change means state money now draws more federal tax credits. One estimate is about $4 from other sources for every state $1.
- The debt is manageable. Bond payments are about 4% of state revenue, close to the historical average.

**Vote NO if you think:**
- Roughly **$630–710K per subsidized unit** (2023 figures from UC Berkeley's Terner Center) is too expensive.
- It won't fix affordability for most people. Shane Phillips of UCLA put it this way: "a bill to produce affordable housing – it is not a bill to make housing affordable." Critics like the Reason Foundation say zoning and permitting reform is the real fix.
- The state shouldn't commit to 25 years of payments when the LAO calls its budget problems "chronic." Prop 38, also on this ballot, would add another $500–600M a year.
- The track record is weak. The 2018 bond produced about 22,700 units against roughly 50,000 promised, though those two counts may not measure the same thing.

**Claims from both sides that don't hold up well:**
- **"It costs roughly double" (Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association):** overstated. Total repayment is about 1.25–1.75 times the amount borrowed, and the LAO says it's about 15% more than paying cash once you adjust for inflation.
- **"No new taxes" (supporters):** true, but the payments come out of the same budget as schools and health care.
- **"Strict accountability" (supporters):** the text I could access mostly requires annual reports and lets the Legislature move money between programs. I didn't find an independent audit requirement for the $10B, but I couldn't read the full measure text.
- **"Veterans" in the title:** only about 11% goes to veterans, and it's the part that costs taxpayers nothing.

**Who's on each side:**
- **Yes:** Newsom, the Democratic Party, labor unions, the California Apartment Association, pro-housing groups SPUR and California YIMBY, and the SF Chronicle and Sacramento Bee.
- **No:** the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, the Reason Foundation, LA Times columnist George Skelton, the OC Register, Mercury News and San Diego Union-Tribune (all the same owner), and several Republican legislators. The state GOP is neutral, and there's no funded No campaign.

**In one line:** if you'd pay $500–600M a year for 25 years to subsidize about 40,000 below-market rentals and help up to 40,000 households buy homes, vote Yes. If you'd rather not add the debt, or think the money is poorly spent, vote No.
