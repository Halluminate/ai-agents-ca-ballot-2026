**Yes.**

This is the $11.25 billion Veterans and Affordable Housing Bond Act of 2026, not the 2024 behavioral health Prop 1. The research leans yes, but it's a judgment about taking on new state debt for housing, so the downsides matter.

**Why yes**
- **The money mostly goes to stalled projects.** Enterprise Community Partners counts nearly 40,000 approved units waiting on financing, per [SPUR](https://www.spur.org/voter-guide/2026-11/ca-prop-1-affordable-housing-bond). A county summary of LAO findings says the 2018 housing bond money is already allocated, so approved projects stall without new funds.
- **The state's cost is modest.** The LAO puts the cost at $500–600 million a year for about 25 years, roughly 0.25% of the General Fund budget. Per the [LAO bond overview](https://lao.ca.gov/ballotanalysis/bonds), bond debt service is about 3% of revenue, below a historical average of about 4%. This adds about a quarter point.
- **The $1.25 billion veterans piece is self-repaying.** Veterans' loan payments cover that debt, and the LAO says the program has "always operated at no direct cost to the state."
- **Support is broad and opposition is thin.** A PPIC October poll, as reported by [WRE News](https://wrenews.com/california-proposition-1-housing-bond-11-25-billion-november-2026/), shows 60% yes and 38% no among likely voters, up from 55% in September. The state guide has no official argument against, and opponents have reported raising no money. Newsom and the [SF Chronicle](https://davisvanguard.org/2026/10/sf-chronicle-endorses-prop-1/) back it.

**Strongest case for no**
- **Long debt on top of a budget gap.** The General Fund repays the $10 billion portion, about $12.5–17 billion in total depending on assumptions. The LAO estimates borrowing costs about 15% more than cash after inflation. The state also has about $40 billion of previously approved bonds not yet sold, and the LAO has projected an ~$18 billion gap for 2026–27.
- **The unit numbers are ceilings, and subsidies are expensive.** "Up to 40,000" units depends on other funding. The $7.2 billion rental share works out to about $180,000 per promised unit (my math). [Scott Alexander's post](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/my-california-ballot-2026) votes no, with reservations, and argues the real count will be lower. Other critics say permitting and construction costs are the real problem. [Hoover](https://www.hoover.org/proposition-lab-proposition-1) is skeptical of bonds as the tool.
- **The veterans framing is weak.** About 11% goes to veterans, and critics call the name a "bait-and-switch."

**What I couldn't verify**
- The bill text (SB 417) was blocked. The summaries I could access describe no audit or oversight mechanism. SPUR points to a 10% extremely-low-income set-aside and reserve requirements, but those are program rules, not oversight. This is the biggest gap.
- The poll figures come from a news report of PPIC's survey, because PPIC's page was blocked. The SF Chronicle endorsement comes from a secondary summary.

If you put heavy weight on limiting new state debt, or think subsidies are the wrong tool for housing, vote No.
