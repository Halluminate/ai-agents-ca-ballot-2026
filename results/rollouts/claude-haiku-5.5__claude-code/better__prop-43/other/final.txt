I won't give you a one-word yes or no on this one. It's a contested political call that turns on values: how much weight you give majority rule and taxpayer protection versus funding for local services. I don't think I should steer a close statewide vote. Here's what the research shows so you can decide.

**What it does**
- Starting January 1, 2027, citizen-initiated local special taxes (earmarked for a specific use) would need two-thirds voter approval instead of a simple majority. That covers new, increased, and extended taxes. Special taxes placed on the ballot by local governments already need two-thirds, and general taxes aren't affected.
- It doesn't cut any existing tax and doesn't reach back to taxes already in place, such as LA's mansion tax.
- It targets a gap left by the 2017 *Upland* ruling. The court didn't squarely decide whether the two-thirds rule applies to citizen initiatives, and roughly two dozen such taxes have passed with less than two-thirds support since, per CalMatters.

**Effects**
- The Legislative Analyst's Office says local tax revenue "could be lower in the future than they otherwise would be." It gives no dollar estimate and calls the actual impact "unknown."
- CalMatters notes that LA's mansion tax (about 58% in 2022) and a Sonoma County wildfire and emergency sales tax (62% in 2024) would both have failed at two-thirds.

**The disagreement**
- **Yes side** (Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, California Taxpayers Association, California Chamber of Commerce, California Republican Party): courts opened a loophole that lets earmarked taxes pass with less than the two-thirds voters originally required, and special interests exploit low-turnout elections.
- **No side** (California Professional Firefighters, California Democratic Party, SEIU Local 1000, Sacramento Bee editorial board): a one-third minority could block majority-backed funding for fire protection, 911, schools, roads, and housing, and the measure doesn't reduce any tax. The compromise's author, Assemblymember Buffy Wicks, is also opposed.

**Polling:** PPIC's October survey (Oct. 16–22, likely voters) found 42% yes, 55% no, and 3% undecided (margin ±3.8 points). In September it was 43% yes, 51% no.

**How to decide**
- Lean yes if your bigger concern is that bare-majority votes in low-turnout elections let narrow interests impose special taxes too easily, and you accept that local revenue may grow more slowly.
- Lean no if your bigger concern is that local services are underfunded and a one-third minority shouldn't be able to block majority-backed taxes.

**Caveats:** The full LAO and PPIC PDFs wouldn't load. The LAO language comes from the Secretary of State's copy of the analysis, and the October poll numbers come from a secondary report of PPIC's survey. The "roughly two dozen" figure comes from CalMatters reporting that I saw only in summaries.
