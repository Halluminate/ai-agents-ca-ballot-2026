I'm not going to pick yes or no for you on this one. It turns on a values question: whether a simple majority should be enough to approve a special tax for a specific local purpose. Informed Californians disagree on that, and the research doesn't settle it. The facts are clear enough to decide quickly, though.

**Today is Election Day.** Polls close at 8 p.m. local time. The sources I checked conflict on mail-ballot postmark rules, so if you haven't mailed yours, drop it at a county drop box or vote in person before 8 p.m. Your county elections office can confirm.

## The decision rule

- **Yes** if you think a special tax (one earmarked for a specific purpose) should need two-thirds approval no matter who puts it on the ballot. Cities, counties, and special districts already need two-thirds for their own special taxes. Prop 43 makes citizen-led ones match.
- **No** if you think a simple majority of voters should be enough to approve a special tax for a specific local purpose, even if a third of voters oppose it.

## What's settled either way

- It starts January 1, 2027, and covers new, increased, or extended special taxes that voters propose. Renewals are included.
- It isn't retroactive. It doesn't affect existing taxes or measures already on this ballot.
- It doesn't change the majority rule for general taxes, doesn't cut any tax, and doesn't cap transfer-tax rates. A citizen-initiated earmarked transfer tax like LA's ULA would need two-thirds, though.
- The Legislature passed it 35-1 in the Senate and 68-2 in the Assembly, as part of a June deal with the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association (HJTA). HJTA dropped a broader initiative that also targeted transfer taxes and would have applied retroactively.
- Its author, Assemblymember Buffy Wicks, now opposes it, calling it "bad policy" even though she wrote the narrower version.
- The Legislative Analyst says local revenue "could be lower in the future than they otherwise would be," but calls the amount unknown. No official dollar estimate exists.
- Measures that passed by majority but would have failed at two-thirds include LA's "mansion tax" (ULA, about 58% in 2022) and Sonoma County's 2024 wildfire sales tax (62%).

## The strongest case for each side

- **Yes:** Consistency. Two-thirds applies when government places a special tax on the ballot, but appellate courts have held it doesn't apply to voter-placed ones. A 2025 appellate ruling upheld ULA on a majority vote. Supporters argue that narrow majorities in low-turnout elections shouldn't set earmarked taxes for everyone.
- **No:** Majority rule and local services. SPUR says the measure would eliminate "one of the few viable ways to raise local revenues." The official opposing argument says, "Even if 66% of voters say yes, Prop. 43 would prevent us from funding desperately needed local services." Opponents include firefighters, teachers, SEIU, the state Democratic Party, the counties' association, SPUR, and the author.

## Polling and money

- Berkeley IGS (September): 31% yes, 40% no, 29% undecided.
- PPIC (October, as reported): 42% yes, 55% no among likely voters, down from 43% and 51% in September. Support is 68% among Republicans and 28% among Democrats. I couldn't open PPIC's page to verify this directly.
- Spending: about $15 million for the measure, most of it from the California Business Roundtable's PAC, and about $6 million against.

## What I couldn't verify

- The official argument text and the Tax Policy Center analysis were blocked, so I relied on summaries that quote them.
- Sources describe the 2017 *Upland* ruling differently. CalMatters says the state Supreme Court didn't settle whether citizen-proposed taxes are covered. The Budget Center says it declined review of the lower-court decisions that found they aren't.
