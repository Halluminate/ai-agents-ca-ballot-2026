I won't pick yes or no for you on this one. Whether Prop 1 is worth it depends on a values question, whether borrowing is the right tool for housing, and reasonable Californians land on both sides. Here's what the research shows so you can decide.

**What it does**
- Authorizes $11.25 billion in state bonds. $10 billion funds housing programs, repaid from the General Fund. $1.25 billion funds veterans' home loans, which borrowers repay.
- The largest piece is $7.2 billion for affordable rentals. The LAO estimates up to 40,000 units, an upper bound that assumes other funding.
- Cost: $500–600 million a year for about 25 years from the General Fund. The state already spends about $6 billion a year repaying bonds, so this adds roughly 8–10%.
- The LAO says borrowing costs about 15% more than paying cash, after inflation.

**Case for yes**
- Money is the bottleneck for projects already permitted. Close to 40,000 affordable units are planned or partly funded but stalled for lack of financing.
- The 2018 housing bond money is spent, and federal support is uncertain.
- Backed by Newsom, the state Democratic Party, the California Apartment Association, AFL-CIO California, the San Francisco Chronicle, and the Sacramento Bee.
- About 60% of likely voters say yes and 38% no in PPIC's October survey.

**Case for no**
- It costs more than paying from the budget, and it locks in payments while the state faces multibillion-dollar shortfalls.
- Critics say zoning and construction costs drive high housing prices, and more money doesn't fix that.
- The "veterans" name is misleading. Veterans get about 11% of the money.
- Opposed by the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, Republican state Sen. Tony Strickland, and, per CalMatters, the OC Register, San Diego Union-Tribune, and Mercury News.
- The state's printed voter guide includes no argument against, so the official pamphlet shows only the yes side.

**How to decide**
Two questions settle it:
1. Should the state borrow about $500–600 million a year for 25 years for housing, rather than fund it from the budget?
2. Is money what's holding housing back, or is it zoning and construction costs?

If you answer "borrow" and "money," you lean yes. If you answer "budget" or "zoning," you lean no.

**Caveats:** I couldn't open the LAO PDF, PPIC's file, or the opposing editorials directly. The figures above match across the sources I could read.
