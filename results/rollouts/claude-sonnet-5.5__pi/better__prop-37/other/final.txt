I'm not going to give a bare yes or no on a live ballot measure. I read the official guide, the LAO analysis, the full text of the measure, and analyses from PPIC, CalMatters, the California Budget & Policy Center, Hoover, and editorial boards on both sides. The independent analysts say the outcome is uncertain, so "better" depends on how you weigh the risks. That's your call.

**What's established**
- Prop 37 lets CalHFA sell up to $25B in revenue bonds to fund second mortgages. Each would cover up to 17% of a newly built home's price.
- Buyers must put down at least 3%, earn no more than 200% of area median income, and buy under a price cap of roughly $1M–$1.5M.
- The LAO finds no direct state or local costs. On paper, bondholders bear default losses. Some analysts doubt lawmakers could resist bailout pressure in a downturn.
- Builders who opt in accept labor standards. In return they get changed construction-defect rules, including a 30% cap on contingency fees.

**What's unknown**
- The interest rate and terms are set after the vote. Second mortgages are riskier for investors, so rates could exceed first-mortgage rates (about 6.5–7%). PPIC found many eligible renters still couldn't afford the monthly payments.
- It's unclear whether investors will buy the bonds or whether the program adds homes. A campaign-commissioned study says up to 190,000, which its author calls the high end.

**Who says what**
- **Yes:** Newsom, the state Democratic Party, the Realtors, carpenters' and nurses' unions, SPUR, the SF Chronicle, and McClatchy's California papers (with reservations).
- **No:** League of Women Voters of California, San Diego Union-Tribune, Southern California News Group papers, and East Bay Times.
- **Their concerns:** more debt for buyers, loose targeting (no first-time-buyer requirement), and possible sprawl. Supporters see the new-construction-only rule as the point, because it adds supply instead of bidding up existing homes.

**How it maps to a vote**
- **Yes** fits if you'd try a program that is low-risk for the state and could ease down-payment barriers, spur construction, and reform defect law, even though it's unproven.
- **No** fits if undetermined loan terms, broad targeting, and the defect-law changes outweigh that. It also fits if you'd rather see a version with clearer terms later.

A Yes locks the program in as a statute that needs a more-than-60% legislative vote to amend. A No doesn't stop the state from trying a similar program later.
