**Yes.**

This is a close call that turns on a values tradeoff, so here is the basis for my pick.

**Why yes**
- The nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) simulated 50 years of budgets. It found today's 10% cap covers only about a third of future funding shortfalls, against roughly half with a 20% cap. The LAO wanted to go further, so this is a step rather than a full fix.
- The LAO called the spending-limit change "reasonable." Reserve deposits stop counting as spending, but withdrawals do count. That changes when money counts against the limit, not the total.
- The measure raises no taxes.

**Best case for No**
- It makes spending-limit (Gann Limit) rebates less likely in boom years. Half of any excess revenue goes to taxpayers and half to schools, so schools' share shrinks too.
- It leaves the loose "budget emergency" rules in place and makes them easier to invoke, because the governor's May budget revision can serve as the declaration. A bigger fund is only as good as lawmakers' discipline. They suspended required deposits in 2024–25 and 2025–26.
- The Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, the California Taxpayers Association (CalTax), the OC Register and the San Diego Union-Tribune oppose it. The SF Chronicle, Sacramento Bee and SPUR support it.

**Both official ballot arguments overstate.**
- It doesn't "double the Rainy Day Fund" today. It doubles the cap, and the fund holds about $15B against a roughly $25B cap that isn't currently binding.
- It doesn't "cancel" rebates or eliminate the spending limit.

If a hard spending limit, or distrust of Sacramento, matters most to you, No is a defensible vote.
