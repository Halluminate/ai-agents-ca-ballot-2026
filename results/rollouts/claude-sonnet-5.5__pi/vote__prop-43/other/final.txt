I'm not going to pick a side for you on this one. I read the measure, the Legislative Analyst's report, the ballot arguments, polls, campaign filings and editorials from both sides. Both sides agree on what the measure does. The disagreement is over priorities, which I don't know, so a bare yes or no from me would be a guess. On a contested ballot measure I'd rather inform your decision than make it.

**Your one-word answer, depending on your view**
- **Yes** if an earmarked local tax should need two-thirds approval whether a city council or a petition drive puts it on the ballot. That means a bit over one-third of voters could block a tax most voters want.
- **No** if a simple majority should be enough when residents qualify a tax by petition. That means some earmarked taxes would pass with less than two-thirds support.

Tell me which sounds more like you, and I'll give you the one word.

**What Prop 43 does and doesn't do**
- **New rule:** From Jan. 1, 2027, new, increased or extended *special* (earmarked) local taxes proposed by citizen initiative would need 66.7% instead of 50%+1.
- **Unchanged:**
  - General (unearmarked) taxes still need only a majority.
  - Earmarked taxes proposed by councils or boards already need two-thirds.
  - Existing taxes stay in place, though renewals or increases would need two-thirds.
  - Every local measure on this year's ballot is decided under the current rule.
- **Transfer taxes:** There is no cap on real-estate transfer taxes. That was in a broader Howard Jarvis initiative, withdrawn in a June 25 deal, and Prop 43 is the narrower replacement.
- **Property tax clause:** A second clause extends the existing 1% property-tax limit to initiatives. Its practical effect is unclear.
- **Permanence and revenue:** It's a constitutional amendment, so only another statewide vote could undo it. The Legislative Analyst says local revenue "could be lower" by an "unknown" amount.
- **Thresholds matter:** When school-bond approval dropped from two-thirds to 55% in 2000, passage rates rose nearly 30%.

**The real dispute**

The question is whether citizen initiatives are a legitimate way for communities to tax themselves, or a loophole.
- **Supporters** say officials and allied groups are closely involved in what are formally "citizens'" initiatives, such as the Bay Area's Measure RTM and L.A. County's Measure A.
- **Opponents** point to measures that would have failed, such as Sonoma County's fire and emergency sales tax (about 62%) and L.A.'s Measure ULA (58%).
- CalMatters reports more than two dozen such taxes have passed under the majority rule since 2017.

**Where each side stretches**
- **Yes:**
  - The "2,000 new taxes" figure and the transfer-tax examples aren't what Prop 43 changes.
  - I found no new "spend it as promised" rule in the text.
  - The "nearly unanimous" legislative vote (68–2 and 35–1) was a deal to avoid a worse initiative. Its author, Buffy Wicks, Gov. Newsom and the state Democratic Party now oppose it.
- **No:**
  - The "$3 billion a year" figure is the campaign's own hypothetical, not a Legislative Analyst number.
  - Earmarked fire and police taxes proposed by local governments already need two-thirds.
  - The "one-third can block two-thirds" point is accurate.
- **Money:** The Yes side's funding is mostly the California Business Roundtable PAC and real-estate firms. The No side's is mostly labor unions.

**Where it stands**
- **Polls:** PPIC (Oct. 16–22) found 42% yes and 55% no. Berkeley IGS (Oct. 19–25) found 36% yes, 46% no and 18% undecided.
- **Supporting:** The Orange County Register, San Diego Union-Tribune, Mercury News and the Republican Party.
- **Opposing:** The Sacramento Bee, Santa Rosa Press Democrat, SPUR and the Democratic Party.
- **Neither:** The S.F. Chronicle said it wouldn't back or oppose it.
