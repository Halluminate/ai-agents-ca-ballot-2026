I'm not going to give you a bare yes or no. I read the official guide, the Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) analysis, the measure's full text, polling, and commentary from both sides. The choice comes down to how you weigh taxes, spending and risk, and the research doesn't settle that for you. A quick way to reach your own answer is at the end.

**What Prop 3 does**
- It makes permanent the 2012 (Prop 30) and 2016 (Prop 55) surcharge on top earners, which is set to expire after 2030.
- Rates stay at 10.3%, 11.3% and 12.3% on income above roughly $371K for single filers and $742K for joint filers. No one's rate changes versus today, and about 98% of filers are below the thresholds.
- The LAO estimates $5–15 billion a year, swinging with the stock market. About 40% goes to schools and community colleges, and the rest goes to other state programs and reserves. The top 2% pay about half of all state income tax.
- It's a constitutional amendment, so changing it later takes another statewide vote.

**Strongest case for Yes**
- It keeps existing rates rather than raising them. If it lapses, top earners get a tax cut, and the California Budget & Policy Center (a left-leaning think tank) estimates about 98% of it would go to the top 1%. The state would also lose $5–15 billion a year, which supporters say matters more with federal cuts coming.
- The number of filers above the top-rate thresholds roughly doubled between 2013 and 2024, from about 185K to about 376K. Supporters cite this against the "exodus" argument, though it doesn't prove no one left.
- Backers include the California Teachers Association (CTA), the state PTA, the California Democratic Party and the California Medical Association. The SF Chronicle, Mercury News and Sacramento Bee editorial boards also back it.

**Strongest case for No**
- It was sold as temporary in 2012 and extended in 2016. California's top rate, 13.3% with the mental-health surtax, is already the nation's highest.
- There's no deadline pressure. The rates run through 2030, so voters could revisit this in 2028 or 2030.
- A 2024 study by Rauh and Shyu found that high earners' responses to the original Prop 30 erased about 45% of the expected new revenue in year one and 61% by year two. Mostly, high earners who stayed reported less income, and about 0.8% left. That study looked at the original increase, not an extension.
- Revenue is volatile, from over $16B in 2021 to about $12B in 2024. Opponents say the state has a spending problem and point to record per-pupil funding of $27,418.
- Opponents include the California Taxpayers Association (CalTax), the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association and the California Hispanic Chambers of Commerce. The OC Register and San Diego Union-Tribune editorial boards also oppose it.

**Claims to treat carefully**
- "Doesn't raise your taxes" and "largest permanent income-tax increase in state history" both depend on the baseline. Versus today, nothing changes. Versus scheduled expiration, it's $5–15 billion a year more.
- "Every dollar goes to classrooms" is only partly accurate. The 89%/11% flows to districts and colleges are locally controlled and audited. But they count toward the Prop 98 school funding minimum, so only about 40% of the revenue becomes added school funding. The rest frees up money for other programs and reserves.
- Layoff and cut figures, like the CTA's, are campaign projections, not LAO estimates. I didn't verify the waste-spending figures in the No argument, and the guide doesn't fact-check either side.

**Where it stands**
- Yes has raised more than $46 million, including $33 million from the CTA. No has raised $5,500, so you've likely heard one side far more than the other.
- Likely-voter polls show 58% yes in the PPIC poll (Oct. 16–22) and 54% yes to 37% no in the Berkeley IGS poll (Oct. 19–25).

**Your yes/no key**
- **Lean Yes** if you're comfortable with the top 2% paying these rates indefinitely and want to lock in the revenue ahead of federal cuts and downturns.
- **Lean No** if you think California's tax burden is already too high, "temporary" should mean temporary, or you're worried about volatility and high earners leaving or reporting less. Also lean No if you'd rather revisit this in 2028–2030.
