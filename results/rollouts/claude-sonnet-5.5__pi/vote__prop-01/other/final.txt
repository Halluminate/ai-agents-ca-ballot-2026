I won't give a bare yes or no. I read the official guide, the LAO analysis, the bill text, and coverage and editorials from both sides. The facts are fairly clear, and what's left is a values call between long-term debt and a real funding gap for affordable housing. I don't think I should make that call for you.

**What Prop 1 does**
- It lets the state borrow $11.25B. Of that, $10B is repaid from the General Fund. Most of it ($7.2B) goes to affordable rental construction and rehab, and the rest to homebuyer help, farmworker, student and tribal housing, and infrastructure. The other $1.25B funds CalVet home loans that veterans repay themselves.
- The nonpartisan LAO says repayment costs the General Fund $500–600M a year for about 25 years, roughly 0.25% of the budget. That is about 15% more than paying cash, adjusted for inflation.
- The LAO says the bond subsidizes up to 40,000 rental units, about 2,500 farmworker units and about 1,200 student beds. It also helps up to 40,000 households toward homeownership.

**Strongest case for Yes**
- The 2018 housing bond is fully committed, and the state's main rental program gets about $9 in requests per $1 available.
- An industry report counts roughly 40,000 permitted affordable units stalled for lack of gap financing. This year's budget put only $200M of new cash into that program.
- Funded units must stay income-restricted for 55 years. Supporters say each state dollar draws about $4 in other money, which is their estimate.
- Backers include Newsom, the Democratic Party, veterans and housing groups, and California YIMBY, a pro-construction group. The SF Chronicle and Sacramento Bee editorial boards say yes. The Bee wants a public cost-per-unit dashboard.

**Strongest case for No**
- It is a 25-year claim on the budget. The LAO projected deficits of $20–35B a year as of January.
- Cost estimates vary. Howard Jarvis says taxpayers repay "roughly double." The Budget Center puts nominal repayment at about $17.4B on $10B. The LAO's inflation-adjusted premium is 15%.
- It doesn't address construction costs. RAND found subsidized affordable housing in California costs about 1.5× market-rate housing here. It cites above-market wage requirements and design-related fees. Forty thousand units is about 4% of the roughly 1 million affordable homes the state says it needs.
- Only 11% goes to veterans, despite the name. The bill's accountability chapter is one annual-report requirement, which supporters call "strict accountability." When the Bee asked, proponents couldn't readily show statewide results from the 2018 bond.
- Howard Jarvis, CalTax, and the Mercury News/East Bay Times editorial boards oppose it. Nine legislators, all Republicans, voted no.

**Where things stand:** There is no organized opposition campaign, and the Yes side has raised more than $12M. PPIC's Oct. 16–22 poll showed 60–38 yes among likely voters. Election Day is Nov. 3, and polls close at 8 p.m.

**How to decide**
Both sides agree the bond doesn't fix construction costs. They disagree on whether that matters. Supporters say "California can do both," meaning fund subsidies now and fix costs too. Critics say the money should wait until costs come down.
- **Lean yes** if you think subsidized low-income housing is worth the cost and a bond is the practical way to pay for it, given the backlog and the lack of other funding.
- **Lean no** if you'd rather not add long-term debt during deficits, want cost and permitting reform first, or want this spending to compete in the annual budget.

If you tell me which of these matter most to you, I'll tell you which way they point.
