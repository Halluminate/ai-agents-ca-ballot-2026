I'm not going to give a one-word answer. This looks like a values call that the facts don't settle, and it splits along party lines. PPIC's final poll had 81% of Democrats and 29% of Republicans in favor. I don't think I should tip the scale in a live election. I read the official guide, the LAO analysis, the bill text, polling, and editorials on both sides.

**The measure:** It authorizes $11.25B in bonds. About $10B is repaid from the General Fund. The LAO puts that at $500–600M a year for about 25 years, roughly 0.25% of the budget. The other $1.25B funds CalVet home loans that veterans repay themselves. It would subsidize up to about 40,000 rental units, plus some homebuyer, farmworker, student, and tribal housing. It creates no new tax, and repayment comes out of the General Fund.

**Best case for yes:**
- Earlier housing bond money has run out.
- About 40,000 permitted affordable units are reportedly stalled for lack of gap financing.
- The money leverages federal tax credits and private capital.
- It passed the Legislature 29–2 and 61–7.
- YIMBY groups, SPUR, and the SF Chronicle back it.

**Best case for no:**
- RAND found subsidized housing in California costs about 1.5× market-rate, and critics say the bond does little about why.
- Forty thousand units is only about 4% of the affordable homes the state needs, by the Mercury News's math.
- It adds to about $80B in existing bond debt, during a structural budget imbalance per the LAO.
- Only 11% goes to veterans, despite the title.
- Howard Jarvis, the Mercury News/East Bay Times editorial boards, and LA Times columnist George Skelton oppose it.

**Rule of thumb:** If subsidized affordable housing is worth about $550M a year in debt service to you, even at high per-unit costs, that points to yes. If you'd rather not add debt, or want the cost drivers fixed first, that points to no. If you tell me which matters more to you, I can say which way it points.
