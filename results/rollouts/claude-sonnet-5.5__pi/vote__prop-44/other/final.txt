**No.**

I read the measure's text, the Legislative Analyst's (LAO) analysis, both ballot arguments, the clinic-commissioned cost study, neutral analyses, polling, and coverage and editorials from across the spectrum. My reasons:

- **Nearly every clinic would start out in violation.** The LAO says clinics average about 80% on health services. An independent Form 990 tally of seven large clinics (not a random sample) found none above 84%. The penalty equals the full shortfall, due within 30 days. The union itself says about $1.7B across the system isn't going to the core mission, which matches the opponents' figure.
- **The design is blunt.**
  - The ratio is measured against revenue, so surpluses and reserves count against a clinic. One large clinic spent 93.5% of its expenses on program services but scores 77.9% because it ran a surplus.
  - The Attorney General can redefine "program services" by bulletin, with no public rulemaking.
  - Any patient can sue, with no good-faith defense, and knowing misreporting can mean jail.
  - It may also miss the union's own examples. The AltaMed art purchases were classified as "program services" under IRS rules, which the measure uses as its starting point.
  - Hoover's neutral analysis flags the same structural problems.
- **The risk falls on safety-net patients during federal Medicaid cuts.** The LAO says clinics that can't comply might close instead. The clinic trade group's study projects 14 to 97 of 202 clinic organizations at risk of closing, depending on assumptions. Treat that as a worst case, because the group is an interested party and the study assumes clinics don't adapt.
- **The sponsor appears to be using it as leverage.** Clinic leaders say SEIU-UHW offered to drop it in exchange for union-organizing concessions. The union denies this, and a federal racketeering suit over it is pending. The LA Times editorial board called it "a failed negotiating tactic."
- **Opposition is broad and crosses party lines.** It includes the California Medical Association, the state pediatrics, family-physician, ob-gyn and emergency-physician groups, the California Hospital Association, the teachers' union, Planned Parenthood Affiliates, both major parties and Gov. Newsom. The LA Times, SF Chronicle, Mercury News and Sacramento Bee editorial boards all say No. The latest PPIC poll (Oct. 16–22) has it at 25% yes and 71% no.

The best case for Yes is that some clinics do pay seven-figure executive salaries or run large surpluses, and public reporting of spending ratios is a good idea. But this is the wrong tool, and a No vote doesn't stop the Legislature from passing a narrower fix.

It's Election Day, and polls close at 8 p.m. No results were posted when I checked.
