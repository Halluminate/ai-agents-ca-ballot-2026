You asked for a one-word answer, but I'm going to pass on picking for you. This is a values call (is public money for campaigns worth it?), and the research doesn't settle it. I can make the choice quick, though.

**What your vote does**
- **A Yes vote** lifts California's 1988 ban, so the state, counties and most cities *may* create public campaign financing. It creates no program and spends nothing itself.
  - The Legislative Analyst puts the state cost at a few hundred thousand dollars a year. Local costs depend on future decisions.
  - Any program would have to require participating candidates to accept spending limits and show broad support, such as many small donors.
  - Programs couldn't use money earmarked for education, transportation or public safety. They also couldn't pay legal fees, fines or a candidate's personal campaign loans.
  - The measure also triples the maximum fine for illegal foreign campaign contributions.
- **A No vote** keeps the ban. Charter cities like LA, SF, Oakland and Berkeley already have programs and are unaffected either way.

**Where each side overreaches**
- **Opponents:**
  - "No limit on spending" is wrong. Participating candidates must accept spending limits, and the measure leaves the amounts to local governments.
  - They have better points. The guardrail covers only *earmarked* money, so general-fund dollars are exposed. Nothing requires independent oversight either.
- **Supporters:**
  - "Elections should be won, not bought" outruns the evidence. Seattle's vouchers brought many more donors and candidates and fewer incumbent wins. But research reviews find no solid evidence yet that public financing reduces special-interest influence or changes policy, and some find more polarization.
  - San Francisco's outside spending was near all-time lows in 2022 and a record $19M in 2024. The races differ, but outside money isn't reliably tamed.
  - They have a better point. Other communities would get the same option charter cities already have.

**Who's where**
- **Yes:** Gov. Newsom, Democratic legislators and the state party, League of Women Voters, Common Cause, ACLU of California Action, and the Sacramento Bee/McClatchy editorial boards.
- **No:** the California Taxpayers Association, Howard Jarvis, the California GOP and Reform California. Also the editorial boards of the SF Chronicle, Mercury News, San Diego Union-Tribune and OC Register. The Chronicle has backed public financing before but says this version lacks guardrails.
- **Late-October polls:** PPIC shows 45% yes and 52% no. Berkeley IGS shows 41% yes, 41% no and 18% undecided.

**A quick way to choose**
- Vote **Yes** if you'd let communities try public financing, accepting that the evidence is mixed and the details come later.
- Vote **No** if you object to tax dollars funding campaigns at all, or you want caps and oversight written in first.
