I'm not going to give a bare yes or no on this one. Whether Prop 43 is "better" depends on how you weigh majority rule against taxpayer protections, and that's a values call. I did the research: the official guide, the Legislative Analyst's (LAO) report, the full text, and coverage and endorsements from both sides. Here is what I found.

**What your vote does**
- **Yes:** Starting Jan. 1, 2027, new, increased or renewed local *special* taxes (earmarked for a specific purpose) proposed by citizen initiative would need two-thirds approval. Right now they need a simple majority under court rulings. Special taxes that city councils or county boards put on the ballot already need two-thirds.
- **No:** Citizen-initiated special taxes keep passing with a simple majority.
- **Either way:** General taxes stay majority-vote, existing taxes aren't voided, and no tax is cut. The LAO says local revenues could end up lower than they otherwise would be, but it can't say by how much.

**The core disagreement**
- **For** (Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, CalTax, the state Republican Party, business groups): The same two-thirds bar should apply no matter who puts a special tax on the ballot. They call the initiative route a loophole that interest groups use to get around Prop 13.
- **Against** (Gov. Newsom, the state Democratic Party, the League of California Cities, the counties association, firefighter and teacher unions): Just over one-third of voters could block what a majority wants. They say that makes it harder to fund fire protection, health care, transit and housing.
- **The author opposes it:** Assemblymember Buffy Wicks, who wrote the amendment, urges a No vote. She says she carried it only to get a broader Howard Jarvis initiative off the ballot.

**Matching it to your values**
- If you'd rather make local tax increases harder to pass, that points to Yes.
- If you'd rather keep majority rule and the initiative route for funding local services, that points to No.
