# My recommendation: **NO**

After researching the official voter guide, the full legal text, the Legislative Analyst's analysis, campaign finance records, fact-checks, and editorial positions, here's what I found and why I'd vote no.

## What Prop 42 actually is

Despite its official framing as the "Retirement and Personal Savings Protection Act," every independent analysis — the LAO, CalMatters, Berkeley IGS — describes Prop 42 as a **countermeasure designed to nullify Prop 40**, the "billionaire tax" (a one-time 5% tax on ~200 Californians worth over $1 billion, funding healthcare). The measure's own text contains a "conflicting measures" clause explicitly voiding Prop 40 if Prop 42 gets more votes. It would also permanently ban any new state tax on the ownership of personal property (stocks, bonds, business interests, art, etc.) and most retroactive taxes.

## Key findings that drove my decision

1. **The problem it advertises doesn't exist.** There is no tax — and no live proposal — on ordinary Californians' 401(k)s, pensions, or savings. California taxes retirement funds at withdrawal (following federal rules), and all six wealth tax bills introduced since 2019 died in the Legislature without a single floor vote. The one real wealth tax on the ballot (Prop 40) applies only to billionaires and *exempts* retirement accounts. CalMatters rated the "they'll expand it to everyone" claim only "partially true," noting it's written into the constitution and expansion would require another vote of the people.

2. **It was bought and paid for by the people it protects.** Prop 42 was drafted and funded by Building a Better California, bankrolled by ~$102 million from Google co-founder Sergey Brin (whose estimated tax bill under Prop 40 is ~$13 billion), plus Doerr, Khosla, and others. Opposition spending is outraising supporters roughly 9-to-1 — one of the most expensive initiative fights in California history.

3. **It's redundant for anyone who opposes the billionaire tax.** If you dislike Prop 40, you can simply vote no on Prop 40. Prop 42's only added value is *permanently* amending the constitution to ban wealth taxation — locking in a policy preference written by and for the ultra-wealthy, during an era of historic (and possibly AI-accelerated) wealth concentration.

4. **Even Prop 40's critics reject Prop 42.** The Sacramento Bee, Mercury News/East Bay Times, SF Chronicle, and the LA Times's Skelton all recommend no — with several saying they oppose the billionaire tax but won't stomach the deceptive poison-pill tactic. Their common thread: don't reward ballot measures that paper over their real purpose by stoking phantom fears about your retirement account.

## The honest case for the other side

If your overriding goal is to make a wealth tax constitutionally impossible forever — and you're comfortable with billionaires writing that rule themselves — then Yes is the coherent vote. Gov. Newsom, the firefighters and building trades unions, and the U-T/OC Register editorial boards take that position, citing capital-flight risk and double-taxation concerns. Those are legitimate arguments about Prop 40 itself, but they're arguments for voting on Prop 40 directly, not for this measure.

**Bottom line: Vote NO.** A measure sold as protecting your savings that actually exists to shield ~200 billionaires from a one-time tax — funded by those same billionaires at a 9-to-1 spending advantage — and that would permanently entrench its drafters' preferences in the constitution, fails the smell test. The straightforward way to oppose the billionaire tax is a no vote on Prop 40, not a deceptive constitutional amendment.
