I've completed extensive research on California Proposition 37 (November 2026) — the Middle-Class Homeownership & Family Home Construction Act. Here's a summary of what I found before giving my answer:

**What it does:** Authorizes up to $25 billion in *revenue bonds* for CalHFA to offer down-payment assistance loans (up to 17% of the purchase price) to middle-income California residents (up to 200% of area median income) buying newly constructed homes below county price caps (~$1M–$1.5M). Buyers put 3% down, must occupy the home, and repay the loans — which repay the bonds.

**Key findings from my research:**
- **LAO confirms no direct taxpayer cost** — bonds are repaid by participating homebuyers, not the state; if investors or borrowers don't materialize, the program simply doesn't scale.
- **Unlike typical down-payment subsidies, it's tied to new construction only**, so the added demand lands where supply can respond — this addresses the classic price-inflation critique (a key reason the SF Chronicle editorial board and SPUR endorsed it).
- **Broad, cross-spectrum support:** Gov. Newsom, State Treasurer, State Controller, Democratic Party, labor unions (carpenters, nurses, teachers), chambers of commerce, Realtors, veterans groups, YIMBY advocates; ~61% in PPIC polling.
- **Criticism is real but mostly about suboptimality, not public harm:** League of Women Voters opposes it (second-mortgage debt burden, unknown rates/terms, weak equity targeting — no first-time-buyer priority, income caps up to ~$300K+); several editorial boards (Sac Bee, SD U-T, East Bay Times) say it won't solve root supply/cost problems; PPIC notes many eligible renters still couldn't afford even entry-level new homes.

**My assessment:** The downside is bounded and voluntary (borne by participating borrowers and bond investors, not taxpayers), while the potential upside — helping tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of families bridge a ~$180,000 down-payment hurdle while incentivizing new home construction in a state with the second-worst homeownership rate — is significant. No formal opposition even submitted a ballot argument or formed a committee. The flaws critics cite (targeting, unknown rates) would limit the program's *effectiveness*, not create taxpayer risk. On balance, the low-risk, supply-linked design makes this worth trying.

**yes**
