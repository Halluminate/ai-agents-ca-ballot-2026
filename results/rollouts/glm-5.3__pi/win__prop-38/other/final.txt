I now have comprehensive data from multiple sources: the official voter guide, PPIC polls (September and October 2026), campaign finance records from the Secretary of State, Berkeley IGS polling, and extensive media coverage. Here is my analysis:

**Proposition 38 (Nov. 3, 2026): $8.4 billion bond for immunology/immunotherapy research**

**Key evidence:**

1. **Polling:** The only public polls testing Prop 38 are PPIC's surveys: September (Yes 55% / No 42%) and the final preelection poll, Oct 16–22 (Yes 57% / No 41%, only 2% undecided). Support *grew* over the final month, giving a 16-point lead a week before Election Day. Notably, PPIC uses the official ballot title — which already includes the critics' best attack line ("half to a single... institute") — so the 57% reflects voters seeing that language.

2. **Campaign finance:** Overwhelming Yes advantage — ~$71.2 million raised (mostly from billionaire Gary Michelson) vs. ~$12,000 for No, with ~$20 million already spent on emotional TV ads (e.g., cancer survivors). The No side has run no ads, relying on letter-writing.

3. **Endorsements:** Rare bipartisan unity — Gov. Newsom, the California Democratic Party *and* California Republican Party, plus the Michael J. Fox Foundation, Alzheimer's Association, ALS Association, American Nurses Association/CA, City of Hope, and other medical groups. No side: a Stanford researcher, the League of Women Voters, and newspaper editorial boards (which historically move few voters against a funded campaign).

4. **Precedent:** California voters approved similar medical research bonds — Prop 71 (2004, stem cells) with 59% and Prop 14 (2020) with 51%. The 2020 measure's narrow win is the strongest cautionary parallel, but Prop 38 polls 5+ points better than Prop 14 did.

5. **Context:** The measure is framed as backfilling Trump administration cuts to federal research (78% of Californians disapprove of Trump), and the midterm electorate skews Democratic — the party whose governor and party apparatus back the measure.

The risks (unanimous editorial opposition, the self-dealing controversy around Michelson's institute, a sour electorate) are real but are not translating into erosion in polling, and the underfunded No campaign has no mechanism to reach voters at scale. With a stable 16-point lead, bipartisan endorsements, and a massive ad advantage, the Yes side is clearly favored.
