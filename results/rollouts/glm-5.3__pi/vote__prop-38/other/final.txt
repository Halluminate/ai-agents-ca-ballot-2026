I've completed extensive research on Proposition 38 — the official voter guide and Legislative Analyst's analysis, investigative reporting from CalMatters, editorial positions from every major California newspaper, positions from good-government groups, the campaign finance records, the track record of California's previous medical research bonds, and the details of the alternative research bond already headed to the 2028 ballot.

# My answer: **NO**

## What Prop 38 does
Authorizes $8.4 billion in state bonds for immunology/immunotherapy research — half as competitive grants, but **half ($4.2 billion) guaranteed to a single unnamed UC-affiliated institute**, costing the General Fund $500–600 million per year for ~20 years.

## Why I land on No

**1. It's structured to funnel $4.2 billion to the measure's own sponsor.** The criteria for the "unnamed" institute (200,000+ sq ft space, affiliation with a UC health system serving 3.5M+ outpatients, a $50M "microbiome research program" donation, etc.) match exactly one entity in California: the California Institute for Immunology and Immunotherapy (CIII), co-founded by billionaire Gary Michelson — who wrote Prop 38 and has supplied virtually all of the ~$76–81 million Yes campaign (the No campaign has raised under $10,000). The Yes campaign's own spokesperson conceded CIII "very likely" qualifies.

**2. The institute isn't ready and would be exempt from transparency laws.** As of September 2026, CIII had just **3 employees** (vs. ~51 planned), no completed facility until late 2028, and ~$100 million unfunded construction costs. Despite receiving billions in public money, the measure explicitly exempts it from the Public Records Act, open-meeting laws, and the Political Reform Act. Three retired nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office analysts broke their tradition of silence to warn it "hijacks the initiative process to steer public funds to a billionaire's philanthropic project, and then hides its tracks."

**3. The "pays for itself" claim is speculative.** The LAO says revenue from discoveries is "uncertain" and could take decades to offset costs. California's own precedent is sobering: the $3 billion stem cell bond (Prop 71) promised billions in royalties and delivered a first royalty check of $190,346 — a negligible return.

**4. There's a better alternative coming.** Gov. Newsom just signed SB 895, placing a $7.5 billion science research bond on the **March 2028 ballot** — competitively awarded through peer review across disciplines, with full transparency protections. Even the No campaign's leaders say the state realistically can't fund both.

**5. Broad expert consensus against it.** Every editorial board I could find opposes it (SF Chronicle, Sacramento Bee, Mercury News/East Bay Times, Fresno Bee, Press Democrat, Orange County Register), along with an unusual left-right coalition: League of Women Voters, Common Cause, and Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association.

## What I weighed on the other side
Immunotherapy research is genuinely promising, federal science funding cuts are real, the cause has sincere backing (Michael J. Fox Foundation, Alzheimer's Association, both political parties, Gov. Newsom), and the grant half of the money is competitively distributed. But you can support medical research without handing $4.2 billion in debt-financed taxpayer money to an untested, three-employee institute created by the ballot measure's own billionaire financier — especially with a cleaner, competitive $7.5 billion alternative already slated for 2028.

**Vote No.**
