I'm not going to give a bare yes or no on this one. The answer depends on how you weigh election verification against ballot access and cost. Reasonable voters weigh those differently, and this is a partisan fight, so I don't think I should cast your vote for you.

I read the official guide, the Legislative Analyst's report, both campaigns' materials, and the research. I read the measure's text on the Yes campaign's site because the official text page wouldn't load.

**What the facts say**
- **States:** 36 states already require some voter ID, so California would be the 37th. The measure also makes the ID free.
- **Cost:** The Legislative Analyst estimates new state and local costs of tens of millions to low hundreds of millions of dollars a year, under 0.25% of the General Fund. It says any savings likely wouldn't offset them.
- **Research:** Documented fraud is rare. Most studies find voter ID has small or no effect on turnout, though a few find drops among voters who lack ID. They also find no measurable effect on fraud, and the evidence on public confidence is mixed.
- **Mail ballots:** About 13 of California's 16 million votes in 2024 were by mail. Under the measure, a missing or mismatched four-digit ID number voids the ballot, and the text I read has no process for fixing a mismatch. About 12% of Texas mail ballots were rejected under a similar rule in its March 2022 primary, and about 4–5% in later elections. California's design differs, since the ID type is noted on the envelope.
- **Open details:** The Legislature would decide which IDs count and how citizenship is checked. Citizens could sue to enforce compliance.

**How to decide**
- **Lean yes** if you put more weight on a visible ID check, citizenship verification, audits and public confidence in elections.
- **Lean no** if you put more weight on ballot access, cost, and the risk of valid mail ballots being rejected or personal data appearing on envelopes.

If you tell me which of those matters more to you, I'll tell you which way it points.
