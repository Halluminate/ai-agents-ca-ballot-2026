I'm skipping the one-word answer. I read the official guide, the Legislative Analyst's analysis, nonpartisan explainers, both sides' arguments and the research on high-earner migration. The choice comes down to values: how much to tax high earners, and whether to lock that in permanently. I don't think I should settle that for you on a partisan tax measure.

**What it does:** It removes the 2031 expiration on the 10.3–12.3% rates for income above about $371k (single) or $743k (joint). The top 2% of filers pay these rates. Nothing changes before 2031, and no rate goes up. If it fails, those incomes drop back to the regular 9.3% top rate in 2031. The Legislative Analyst estimates $5–15 billion a year, swinging with the stock market. About 40% goes to schools and community colleges, and the rest goes to other programs and reserves.

**Reasons to vote yes**
- It keeps a revenue source the state has used since 2012 and avoids a $5–15 billion annual hole after 2030.
- It doesn't raise anyone's current rate.
- The evidence for a high-earner exodus is disputed. SPUR, which endorses yes, cites little evidence of one.

**Reasons to vote no**
- The 2012 tax was sold as temporary. This puts permanence in the constitution, so only another statewide vote could undo it.
- California's top rate (13.3% above $1M with the mental health surtax) is already the highest in the country. The budget also leans on a small group of volatile earners.
- Some research (Rauh and Shyu) found that moves and behavior changes erased a large share of the expected first-year revenue from the 2012 increase. Other studies found small effects.
