I'm going to skip the one-word answer. This is a live election, and Prop 1 comes down to a values call (housing need versus long-term debt) that more research can't settle for you. Here's what I found and where the decision hinges.

**What it does:** It authorizes $11.25B in bonds.
- $10B goes to affordable-housing programs, repaid from the General Fund.
- $1.25B goes to veterans' home loans, repaid by the veterans.
- The Legislative Analyst (LAO) estimates $500–600M a year for about 25 years, roughly 0.25% of the General Fund.
- It would subsidize up to 40,000 rental units and help up to 40,000 homebuyers.
- No ballot argument against it was filed.

**Strongest case for**
- The 2018 housing bond was fully committed by the end of 2023. The main program is oversubscribed about 9 to 1, and about 46,000 approved affordable homes are waiting on the last state funding.
- Paying cash hasn't been the recent pattern. The 2026 budget has only $200M for that program and $500M for state tax credits.
- Bonds suit long-lived buildings. The state also recently consolidated its housing finance programs and moved on permitting and fee reforms.

**Strongest case against**
- It's debt: about $17.4B to repay $10B. That figure is nominal. The LAO puts the premium at about 15% over paying cash, after inflation. It adds to roughly $70–80B of existing bond debt, roughly $38–40B authorized but unsold, and Prop 38's separate bond.
- Much of the money goes to loans, grants and down-payment aid, not only buildings. The Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association objects that such programs belong in the annual budget.
- It doesn't address why affordable housing costs so much to build. The Sacramento Bee endorsed yes but called for per-unit cost data to be published.
- The bill requires an annual report. I found no independent audit requirement for the housing programs. It also lets the Legislature amend program rules and shift money between programs.
- "Veterans" is in the title, but they get about 11% of the money, and they repay it themselves.

**Who's where**
- **For:** Gov. Newsom, the Democratic Party, major unions, the California Apartment Association, SPUR, and the SF Chronicle and Sacramento Bee editorial boards.
- **Against:** Howard Jarvis, Reform California, some Republican legislators (the Assembly vote was 61–7), and the OC Register and Press Democrat editorial boards.
- The state GOP is neutral, and there's no funded opposition campaign.

**The crux:** If Prop 1 fails, does this housing get funded some other way?
- If you expect the Legislature would pay from the General Fund, a No gets you the same housing without the financing premium.
- If you expect it wouldn't, a No means less affordable housing gets built.
- The record is mixed. LAist reports about $8B of General Fund housing money since 2019, but the 2026 budget is far smaller.

**How it breaks down**
- **Yes fits if** you think the shortage justifies borrowing now, accept the interest premium for speed, and trust the state to deliver the units.
- **No fits if** you'd rather not add long-term debt for program spending, want cost and permitting problems fixed before more subsidy, or want stronger audit controls first.

**Limits of my research:** CalMatters lists the Mercury News and San Diego Union-Tribune as opposed, but I couldn't read either editorial.
