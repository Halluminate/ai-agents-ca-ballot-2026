I'm not going to give a bare `yes` or `no` on this one. After reading the LAO, PPIC, SPUR, the League of Women Voters, the California Budget & Policy Center and the editorial boards, I found the evidence genuinely split. The answer turns on what you value, so a one-word verdict from me would overstate my confidence.

**What it does:** CalHFA could sell up to $25B in revenue bonds to fund second mortgages. Each loan covers up to 17% of the price, with 3% down. Buyers must earn up to 200% of area median income and buy a newly built home under roughly $1–1.5M. Builders who accept labor standards get modified construction-defect rules. The LAO finds no direct state or local costs.

**Strongest case for yes:**
- Bondholders, not taxpayers, bear losses. If investors don't buy the bonds, the outcome is "potentially nothing."
- Limiting loans to new construction ties the subsidy to supply.
- The defect rules could revive condo building.
- SPUR, the SF Chronicle and the Democratic Party back it, and PPIC's October poll has it at 65% yes.

**Strongest case for no:**
- The loan rate and monthly payment aren't known until after the vote. At 7%, the second mortgage could add about $1,000 a month.
- PPIC found fewer than 40% of eligible renters could afford the payments on an entry-level home.
- There's no first-time-buyer rule, so benefits may skew toward higher earners.
- The subsidy could be absorbed into higher prices.
- Homeowners get weaker defect remedies.
- The League of Women Voters, the OC Register and the San Diego Union-Tribune oppose it.
- There is no organized opposition campaign, which is why the official guide has no "against" argument.

**How to decide:** If you're comfortable with a low-taxpayer-risk experiment and like the construction and defect-reform angle, that leans yes. If you distrust demand-side subsidies, the loose targeting or the defect-law changes, that leans no. Tell me which matters most to you and I'll tell you which way it points.
