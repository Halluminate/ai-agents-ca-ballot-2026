**No.**

It's a values call, but here's my basis, using the full measure text and coverage through Nov. 2, 2026:

- **The court limits are the real change, not the deadlines.** Challengers could only argue violations of "objective" existing laws. Courts could halt only the noncompliant part of a project, not rescind an approval. The rules would also apply to lawsuits already pending.
- **Public input shrinks under the new process, even for projects nobody sues.** Only about 2% of CEQA projects get sued each year, per the Little Hoover Commission. But comment periods are capped at 20 or 45 days, most late comments are excluded from the record, and applicants who use the fast track can limit the analysis to one alternative of their own design.
- **Benefits aren't guaranteed, and the scope is broad.** "Essential" covers market-rate housing and related infrastructure, and nothing requires affordability or lower prices. The "$75,000 per home" savings figure comes from supporters. Whether data centers qualify is disputed.
- **It's hard to undo.** Amendments require a two-thirds legislative vote and must further the measure's purposes. Support has also dropped from 73% in July to 46% in PPIC's final poll (50% no).

**The case for yes:** CEQA delays are real. The measure doesn't exempt projects from environmental review, local agencies keep approval power, and most non-housing projects would face labor standards, including prevailing wages for private developers. If faster housing, water, clean energy, and wildfire projects matter more to you than weaker review and court access, yes is a defensible vote.

Some provisions are ambiguous, such as whether the court limits apply to every eligible project or only to applicants who opt in. Courts would have to settle that after passage.
