**No.**

The research goal is worthwhile. The problem is how this measure is built:

- **Half the money is effectively earmarked for one institute.** The eligibility rules appear to fit only the California Institute for Immunology & Immunotherapy at UCLA. UCLA confirmed that no other UCLA-affiliated nonprofit qualifies, and the Yes campaign says the institute is "very likely" to qualify. Its board chair, Gary Michelson, has put more than $60 million into the Yes campaign. The No side has reported about $12,000.
- **Weak public oversight.** The institute would be exempt from the Public Records Act, the open-meetings law, and the Political Reform Act. It would also be the "clearinghouse and approval center" for licensing deals from all bond-funded research, and the grant council must consult it on funding decisions.
- **It isn't ready.** As of September it had three employees, against a plan of 51. Its building isn't expected to be fully built until late 2028 and still needs about $100 million.
- **The cost is certain; the payback isn't.** The Legislative Analyst estimates $500–600 million a year for about 20 years from the General Fund, roughly $10–12 billion total. Earlier estimates said 25 years. Any royalty return is uncertain and could take decades. California's earlier stem-cell bonds ($8.5 billion) have returned little: the first royalty check, about $190,000, arrived more than 13 years in.
- **Better options exist.** SB 895, a broader $7.5 billion research bond, is already set for the March 2028 ballot. The federal-cuts case is mixed: Congress gave NIH a 1% increase for FY2026 after rejecting a proposed 40% cut, though grant freezes did hit some California labs.

The San Francisco Chronicle, Sacramento Bee, Mercury News/East Bay Times, and OC Register all urge No.

**Best case for Yes:** Immunotherapies already treat some cancers, the annual cost is about 0.25% of the General Fund, and the measure includes a 2% administrative cap, audits, and a 20% California price discount. Newsom, both state parties, and many patient groups support it, and 57% of likely voters did in PPIC's October poll. If you prioritize immunology research and accept the governance problems, Yes is defensible.
