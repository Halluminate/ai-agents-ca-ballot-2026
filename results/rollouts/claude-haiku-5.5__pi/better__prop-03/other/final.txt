I won't give a one-word pick on this one. It turns on values: how much to weigh school and health funding against taxes on the top 2%, and whether to lock the rate in now. Here's what the research shows so you can decide.

**Facts both sides accept**
- **Yes** makes the top state rates permanent: 10.3–12.3% on income above roughly $371K (single) or $743K (joint), or 13.3% with the 1% mental-health surcharge, the nation's highest. **No** lets them expire in 2031, when the top rate falls to 9.3%.
- The Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) estimates the tax brings in $5–15 billion a year. That amount is volatile because it tracks the stock market. The top 2% already pay about half of state income tax.
- About 40% goes to K-14 schools (89% K-12, 11% community colleges). The rest goes to other state programs and reserves, including up to $2 billion a year for children's health care. The yes campaign acknowledges that the freed-up money goes to Medi-Cal.
- It would be written into the constitution, so later changes would generally need voter approval.
- The state faces deficits. The LAO projects $20–30 billion a year in structural shortfalls and notes General Fund spending has risen about $100 billion since 2019–20.

**Where the disagreement is**
- **"Tax increase" or not?** It doesn't raise current rates. Compared with 2031, it keeps $5–15 billion a year that would otherwise disappear. Both labels are defensible, depending on the baseline.
- **Do high earners leave?** Stanford/Hoover economist Joshua Rauh found that departures and income shifts after Prop 30 erased almost half the expected revenue gain. Other researchers found only a "very slight difference" in migration, and Rauh argues they undercounted movers. It's unresolved.
- **Spending vs. revenue:** Opponents say the state should fix spending first. Supporters say cuts would hit schools and Medi-Cal without this money.
- **Small business:** Opponents say many pass-through businesses report income on personal returns and would be hit too. Supporters say the tax applies only to the top 2%.

**The decision turns on**
1. Would losing $5–15 billion a year from 2031 hurt programs you care about more than keeping the top rate costs high earners?
2. Do you expect high earners' responses to shrink the revenue more than estimated?
3. Are you comfortable locking this in five years before it expires?

Sources: [LAO analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=3&year=2026), [official voter guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/3/), [CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/politics/elections/2026/10/prop-3-california/), [Stanford GSB on Rauh and Shyu](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/has-california-hit-limit-tax-rates).
