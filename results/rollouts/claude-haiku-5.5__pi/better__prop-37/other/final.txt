I won't give a one-word yes or no on this one. Whether yes or no is better depends on how you weigh real down-payment help against a second monthly loan and some unresolved risks, and informed people land on both sides. I reviewed the official guide, the LAO analysis, the measure text, PPIC and CalMatters analyses, and the major editorial positions. Here's what matters most.

**What it does**
- Lets the state sell up to $25 billion in bonds to fund second mortgages covering up to 17% of a new home's price. Buyers still put 3% down, earn up to 200% of area median income, and repay the second loan monthly.
- The Legislative Analyst finds no direct state or local cost, since borrowers repay the bonds. The measure says the bonds are not state debt.

**Case for yes**
- It targets the down payment, often the real barrier. PPIC found about 90% of renters would meet the income limit.
- It covers only newly built homes and gives builders a path to fewer defect lawsuits, aiming to add supply.
- Backers include Gov. Newsom, the California Democratic Party, the Realtors, carpenters' unions, and the San Francisco Chronicle's editorial board.

**Case for no**
- The rate, term, and monthly payment are set after the vote. Second mortgages are riskier for investors, so rates could be high. At 7% over 30 years, a $136,000 loan is about $900 a month.
- "Below-market" is not guaranteed. It depends partly on foundations buying bonds at about 3%. Otherwise the bonds would sell at market rates.
- PPIC estimates that, in 2024 and before any second loan, 56% of renters couldn't afford an entry-level new home under a 36% debt-to-income limit.
- Limiting aid to new homes may not add enough supply and could push prices up. The LAO lists investor demand, loan costs, and whether construction actually rises as open questions.
- Builders who opt in get a modified defect-repair process, including a path to dismissal if homeowners stall, in exchange for accepting liability for contractors' labor violations. The measure also caps attorney contingency fees in defect suits at 30% (courts can approve more). It exempts these loans from Civil Code § 2924.13, a 2025 law that adds protections for second-mortgage borrowers facing foreclosure.
- The League of Women Voters, the San Diego Union-Tribune, and the Southern California News Group and East Bay Times editorial boards oppose it. No opposing committee has reported money. The Yes side has raised more than $30 million, mostly from Realtors.

**How to decide**
- Yes fits if you can afford a second monthly payment, think the program will add homes rather than raise prices, and accept the builder-liability changes.
- No fits if the monthly cost and unknown terms worry you, you doubt the supply effect, or you prefer targeted programs like CalHFA's existing loans, which defer repayment but have tighter eligibility.
