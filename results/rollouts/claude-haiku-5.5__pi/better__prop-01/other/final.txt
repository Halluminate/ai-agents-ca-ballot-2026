I won't give you a one-word yes or no, even though you asked for one. Whether to borrow $11.25 billion for housing depends on values: how much near-term housing is worth decades of debt payments, and whether bonds or cost reforms are the better fix. Informed people and newspaper boards split on it. I read the official guide, the LAO analysis, and coverage and endorsements from both sides.

**What it does**
- Authorizes $11.25 billion in state bonds for housing, on the Nov. 3, 2026 ballot.
- $10 billion is repaid from the General Fund: about $500–600 million a year for roughly 25 years, about 0.25% of the budget. The LAO estimates the total cost is about 15% more than paying cash, after inflation.
- $1.25 billion goes to veterans' home loans, repaid by the veterans themselves.
- Targets up to 40,000 rental units and 40,000 homebuyer households, plus farmworker, student, tribal, and infrastructure money.

**Case for yes**
- About 40,000 affordable units are designed, permitted, and partly funded but stalled for lack of money (CalMatters, citing Enterprise Community Partners).
- The 2018 housing bond is largely committed. The LAO reports no discretionary state housing funding in 2024–25, and $641 million was reverted.
- Backers include Gov. Newsom, Democrats, housing groups, unions, and cities. The San Francisco Chronicle and Sacramento Bee endorsed it. Supporters say it adds no new taxes.

**Case for no**
- It adds decades of General Fund payments, which compete with schools and health care, while the state faces projected budget deficits.
- Critics say it doesn't address why building costs so much. A 2025 RAND study found subsidized housing in California costs about 1.5 times market-rate housing.
- Only about 11% goes to veterans despite the name. Republicans, who cast every no vote in the Legislature, call that misleading and want regulatory reform first. The state GOP is officially neutral.
- The Mercury News/East Bay Times, Press Democrat, Daily Breeze, and San Diego Union-Tribune urged no.

**Other context**
- Organized opposition is thin. As of late October, no committee had registered against it, and the official guide includes no opposing argument.
- CaliforniaPeople.org, a third-party voter guide, calls it a toss-up. It cites high per-unit costs, past state audit findings on the housing agency, and the lack of a per-unit cost cap in the bill.

**What should decide it**
- Vote yes if you think borrowing now to finish stalled projects is worth the annual payments, even without fixing construction costs.
- Vote no if you think the state should fix costs and permitting first and avoid new debt.

The LAO's nonpartisan analysis is the best starting point for the facts: lao.ca.gov/ballot/2026/prop1-110326.pdf
