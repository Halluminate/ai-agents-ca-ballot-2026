I won't give a one-word yes or no on this one. It's a contested call that depends on trade-offs reasonable people weigh differently. Here's what the research shows.

**What the record shows**
- **Cost:** An $8.4 billion bond. The Legislative Analyst (LAO) estimates repayment at $500–600 million a year for about 20 years (some critics cite 25), roughly 0.25% of the General Fund each year. A 10% share of licensing revenue could offset part of that, but the LAO calls the timing and amount uncertain.
- **Half to one institute:** The $4.2 billion half goes to one nonprofit chosen by the state Department of Public Health. The eligibility criteria appear to fit only the California Institute for Immunology and Immunotherapy at UCLA. UCLA confirmed it's the only UCLA-affiliated nonprofit that qualifies, and the Yes campaign says it's "very likely" to. Its co-founder and board chair, Gary Michelson, is a chief backer of the measure and has given at least $61 million to the campaign.
- **Transparency:** The institute is exempt from the Public Records Act, state open-meetings law, and the Political Reform Act. Supporters say private institutes shouldn't be bound by those laws and point to required public reports and audits.
- **Readiness:** As of September, the institute reportedly had three employees, against a plan to hire 51 by 2025. The campaign says it's operational and has already administered grants.
- **Amendments:** The Legislature can change the measure only by a two-thirds vote, and not in ways that alter how the bond money is allocated.

**The case for yes**
Federal grant cuts have disrupted research, and this would create a dedicated, stable funding source. It caps administrative costs at 2%, requires audits, returns 10% of licensing revenue to the state, and requires a 20% discount for Californians on resulting treatments. Governor Newsom, the state Democratic and Republican parties, and many patient groups back it. A PPIC poll in October found 57% of likely voters in favor and 41% opposed.

**The case for no**
Half the money goes to one institute tied to its main funder, and that institute is exempt from public-records law. Critics say bonds usually fund long-lived infrastructure, though California has funded stem-cell research with bonds before (Propositions 71 and 14). The revenue offset is uncertain. The Sacramento Bee, OC Register, and East Bay Times editorial boards recommend voting no. The No campaign had raised about $12,000 by October 22, against more than $71 million for Yes. The American Association of Immunologists' policy committee recommended dropping its endorsement, though its council kept it.

**What would decide it for you**
- Is a fast, dedicated research fund worth a single-institute structure and a public-records exemption?
- Should research be funded through the regular budget rather than through bonds and ballot measures?
- How much do you believe the royalty share will repay the bond?
