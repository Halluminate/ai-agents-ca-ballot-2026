I won't give you a yes or no on this one. Whether to lift the ban depends on how you weigh taxpayer risk against campaign-finance reform, and well-informed people land on both sides. I can give you what you need to decide quickly. My sources run through Election Day and don't include results; the Secretary of State's site will have them.

**What the measure does**
- It repeals California's 1988 ban on public money for campaigns, for the state and most local governments. Charter cities such as Los Angeles, San Francisco, Oakland, Berkeley and Long Beach are already exempt and run programs.
- By itself it creates no program and spends no money. Each government would have to create its own later.
- Any program must exclude money earmarked for education, transportation or public safety. Candidates must accept spending limits and show small-donor support. Public money can't pay legal fees or fines or repay a candidate's own loans. Programs can't favor parties or incumbents.
- It sets no dollar amounts and doesn't cover federal races. The Legislature can adjust some details by a two-thirds vote, but the core rules would need another statewide vote. It needs a simple majority.

**Case for yes**
- Only a handful of cities can do this now, and campaigns are very expensive. Tom Steyer alone put $213 million of his own money into this year's governor's race, the most expensive in U.S. history.
- A 2022 study found Seattle's voucher program increased the number of candidates by 86%. Another study found Arizona and Maine's programs significantly increased competition.
- Each community decides for itself, and the measure includes specific guardrails.
- Supporters include the League of Women Voters, the California Nurses Association, Common Cause, the state Democratic Party, Gov. Newsom, and the Sacramento and Fresno Bees.

**Case for no**
- Taxpayer money could fund candidates and messages some taxpayers oppose. The California Taxpayers Association also raises First Amendment concerns.
- The measure is thin on specifics. It sets no dollar caps or qualifying thresholds, and the state's campaign watchdog isn't responsible for local programs. Critics say the officials who'd benefit would write the rules.
- Costs are open-ended. The state's direct cost is a few hundred thousand dollars a year, but the Legislative Analyst says local program costs "could be significant."
- Candidates could still take private and special-interest money.
- The evidence is mixed. A GAO study found no evidence that the Arizona and Maine programs led to more candidates. An Oakland study found no evidence its program reduced wealthy donors' influence.
- Voters approved the ban in 1988 with 58% and rejected repeal efforts in 2000, 2006 and 2010 (26–43% yes). But those measures also created specific programs, which Prop 4 does not.
- Opposing editorial boards include the San Francisco Chronicle, San Diego Union-Tribune, Orange County Register, Mercury News and Daily News.

**Where it stands**
- PPIC (Oct. 16–22): 45% yes, 52% no.
- Berkeley IGS (Oct. 19–25): 41% yes, 41% no, 18% undecided.
- Both have ticked toward yes since September, but neither is above 50%.
- The main yes committee has raised about $750,000. Opponents have no registered committee, and outside groups have spent about $55,000 against the measure.

**Two claims to discount**
- Supporters say California is the only state with this ban. I couldn't verify that, and NCSL notes that Colorado also bars using public money to support a candidate's campaign.
- Opponents say there's "no limit" on campaign spending. That overstates it. Participating candidates must accept spending limits; the amounts are left to future programs.

**How to decide**
Vote yes if you're comfortable letting state and local governments design and police programs that don't exist yet, and you think the taxpayer-money trade-off is worth it. Vote no if the missing specifics, or the use of public money, is a dealbreaker for you.
