I won't give a one-word verdict on this one. Whether Prop. 41 is a good idea depends mostly on how you view the billionaire tax (Prop. 40) and how you weigh audit transparency against spending-cap flexibility. That's a value judgment, so I'll lay out the evidence and let you decide.

**What it does**
- Requires a State Auditor review of programs funded by any citizen-proposed special tax once backers reach 25% of the needed signatures. The summary goes in the voter guide and must recommend ways to cut program costs by at least 10% a year. Approved special taxes get audits every four years.
- Bars new state taxes enacted or taking effect after Jan. 1, 2026 from being exempt from the 1979 Gann spending limit.
- If it gets more yes votes than Prop. 40 and both pass, it can nullify Prop. 40, though courts would resolve the conflict.
- The LAO says the net fiscal effect is unknown. Audit costs are likely in the low millions per year.

**Strongest case for yes**
- Voters get independent performance information before approving special taxes, plus ongoing reviews. Even the Mercury News board wrote that the audit idea "could be valuable."
- Supporters say new taxes shouldn't escape a voter-approved cap, and that the 10% item is a recommendation, not a mandate.
- Backers include CalCPAs, CalTax, the CalChamber, the California Republican Party, and the San Diego Union-Tribune and OC Register.

**Strongest case for no**
- The spending-cap provision is broad and permanent. The League of Women Voters, SPUR, the SF Chronicle, the Sacramento Bee, and the Mercury News/East Bay Times all oppose it, citing future revenue flexibility.
- A law-professor memo warns it could reach existing Medi-Cal financing (the MCO tax and hospital fee) and Prop. 3. Its authors partly drafted Prop. 40, and the state would likely contest those readings.
- Critics say the audits duplicate the State Auditor's and LAO's existing work and that the 10% target is arbitrary. The campaign is funded largely by Google co-founder Sergey Brin's group, and opponents call it a poison pill.
- The cap isn't binding now. The LAO projects about $27 billion of room in 2026–27, so the main risk is future budgets.

**How Prop. 40 changes the math**
- If you want the billionaire tax to take effect, vote no on 41. A yes that outpolls Prop. 40 could void it.
- If you want Prop. 40 to fail, a no on 40 does that by itself. A yes on 41 would then add its audit and spending-cap rules, which take effect if it passes.

I couldn't find election results, so this is pre-election information. The latest polls have 41 short of a majority: PPIC had 48% yes and 49% no (Oct. 16–22), and Berkeley IGS had 38% yes, 47% no, and 16% undecided (Oct. 19–25).
