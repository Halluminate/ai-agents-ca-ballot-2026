I'm not going to give a one-word answer on this one. Whether Prop 42 is the better choice depends on values: whether you want the 5% billionaire tax (Prop 40) to take effect, and whether you want a permanent constitutional ban on new taxes on financial assets. I read the measure text, the LAO analysis, recent polls, and endorsements from both sides. Here's what matters so you can decide quickly.

**A yes vote**
- Bars new state taxes on owning retirement accounts, pensions, stocks, business interests, intellectual property, and other personal property, for taxes enacted after Jan. 1, 2026. Existing taxes and real estate are excluded.
- Bars retroactive taxes, with narrow emergency exceptions.
- Is written to void Prop 40 if Prop 42 gets more yes votes than Prop 40. The LAO notes that courts would have to decide whether the two conflict.
- Changing it would take another statewide vote.

**A no vote**
- Leaves the state free to create these taxes later, through the normal legislative or voter process.
- Doesn't void Prop 40. Prop 40 takes effect if it gets more yes votes than Props 41 and 42.

**Disputed points**
- Prop 40 generally excludes real estate, pensions, and retirement accounts (LAO). CalMatters reports there is no current proposal to tax 401(k)s. Yes backers cite six recent proposals and past asset-tax bills that stalled.
- The LAO says Prop 42 could reduce future tax revenue, by an amount it calls unclear.
- Support is close and slipping. PPIC (Oct. 16–22) had it at 51% yes, 47% no. Berkeley IGS/LA Times (late October) had it at 43% yes, 45% no. Prop 40 trails in both polls, so if it fails on its own, Prop 42's remaining effect is the permanent ban.
- Backers say the measure isn't aimed at Prop 40. Critics call it a poison pill.

**Coalitions**
- Yes: Gov. Newsom, California Professional Firefighters, State Building & Construction Trades Council, CalChamber, and police and veterans' groups. About $128M in reported contributions through Oct. 22, mostly via Building a Better California, which Sergey Brin and other tech billionaires fund.
- No: California Democratic Party, SEIU-UHW (the union behind Prop 40), Bernie Sanders, and the SF Chronicle and Sacramento Bee editorial boards. About $23M in reported contributions.

**How to decide**
- If you want the billionaire tax to take effect, a yes on 42 (or 41) could void it.
- If you oppose the billionaire tax, a no on 40 is the direct way to stop it. Prop 42 matters only if 40 still wins a majority, and a yes on 42 also adds the permanent ban.
- If you're weighing the ban alone: do you want a permanent constitutional limit on future taxes on financial assets?
