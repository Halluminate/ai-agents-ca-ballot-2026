I've now reviewed the official voter guide, the Legislative Analyst's analysis, the pro/con arguments, CalMatters and LA Times coverage, the major editorial board endorsements, and the research on millionaire migration. Here's my assessment:

**What Prop 3 (Nov. 2026) does:** Makes permanent the high-income tax rates (on individuals earning over ~$371K, couples over ~$742K) first passed temporarily in 2012 (Prop 30) and extended in 2016 (Prop 55). It does not raise anyone's taxes — it just prevents rates from expiring in 2031, preserving $5–15 billion/year, with 89% to K-12 schools, 11% to community colleges, plus rainy-day fund deposits and health care support.

**Key points from research:**
- A "no" vote means a $5–15B annual revenue hole starting 2031, hitting schools (California already has the nation's most crowded classrooms, 1:22 vs. 1:15 national average) and Medi-Cal, which is already strained by federal cuts.
- The revenue is volatile, but the measure directs excess funds to budget reserves to smooth downturns.
- Migration research on taxing high earners is mixed, but since this measure *maintains* rather than raises rates, the marginal flight effect vs. the status quo is negligible.
- Broad support: teachers, nurses, firefighters, PTA, Newsom, Becerra — and even the billionaire-funded group fighting the separate Prop 40 wealth tax endorses Prop 3 because it only keeps existing rates.
- Major independent editorial boards — SF Chronicle, Sacramento Bee/McClatchy, Mercury News/East Bay Times — all endorse **yes**, calling it the most important tax measure for California's budget future. The "no" case rests mainly on the philosophical objection that "temporary" taxes shouldn't become permanent, not on the practical effects of losing the revenue.

Weighing the actual fiscal impact, the fact that rates don't change for 98% of taxpayers, the built-in reserves, and the near-universal serious endorsement versus a minimal $5,500 opposition, the balance clearly favors voting yes.
