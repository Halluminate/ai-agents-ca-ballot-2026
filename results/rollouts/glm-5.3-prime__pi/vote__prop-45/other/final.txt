# No.

**Proposition 45 (November 3, 2026)** — the California Chamber of Commerce–sponsored initiative that would create an optional, expedited CEQA review track for projects it defines as "essential" (housing, water, clean energy, healthcare, transportation, broadband, schools, wildfire mitigation).

## Why I'd vote No, after weighing everything

**1. The right goal, the wrong vehicle.** CEQA genuinely needs reform — decades of NIMBY weaponization, union "greenmail," and decade-long lawsuits (e.g., UC Berkeley's People's Park, the 15-year Richardson Grove case) have made California unaffordable. But this is a 41-page rewrite of hyper-technical environmental law locked into statute that can only be amended by a two-thirds legislative vote. Even UC Davis law professor Chris Elmendorf, a leading CEQA expert, says "some of the changes are good and some may be bad, and who knows what second-order effects they will have."

**2. It's streamlining for sprawl, not just housing.** The measure fast-tracks freeway expansions and greenfield development with "ancillary infrastructure," lets the *applicant* develop the only project alternative, freezes environmental standards at the date of application, and was poll-tested to exclude high-speed rail and the Delta tunnel (the Yes campaign's own strategist admitted this). The NYT, Sierra Club, and even pro-density groups like SPUR flagged this as subsidizing exactly the car-dependent, fire-zone fringe growth CEQA was written to stop. Notably, many longtime CEQA reformers — Tom Steyer, SPUR, Gov. Newsom — declined to endorse it.

**3. Real accountability rollbacks, not just deadlines.** It caps public comment at 45 days and excludes late comments from the court record, narrows tribal consultation to only federally recognized tribes, prevents courts from halting whole projects (only non-compliant portions), allows just one round of litigation, and instructs judges to give "the fullest possible weight" to approving projects. And it's ambiguous on whether data centers qualify — likely to spawn the very litigation it claims to curb.

**4. The fiscal math is shaky.** The nonpartisan Legislative Analyst estimates initial costs for state/local governments in the high tens of millions, potentially exceeding $100 million annually, with uncertain long-term effects. The rosy "$75,000 savings per home" figures come from a study the campaign itself commissioned. Funding is dominated by utilities, developers, tech billionaires, and oil money (California Resources Corp gave $1M+).

**The honest counterargument:** The San Diego U-T, Sacramento Bee, Mercury News, OC Register, and ~28 other papers endorse it, backed by children's hospitals, the NAACP, LULAC, and affordable-housing builders — arguing the Legislature has dithered for 50 years and enforceable deadlines are overdue. That case is real. But Sacramento has now passed major CEQA reforms in 2024–2025 (including exempting most infill housing), and this measure's worst provisions — sprawl fast-tracking, frozen standards, weakened tribal and public input — fall hardest on exactly the projects CEQA exists to scrutinize, while its benefits accrue to its corporate funders.

**Bottom line: No.**
