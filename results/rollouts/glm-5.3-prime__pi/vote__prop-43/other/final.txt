# No.

## What Prop 43 actually does

Prop 43 would amend the California Constitution so that local "special taxes" (taxes earmarked for a specific purpose) proposed by **citizen initiative** must get **two-thirds approval**, instead of the current simple majority — starting January 1, 2027. Taxes proposed by city councils/boards already need two-thirds; the courts (in the *Upland* case and follow-ups from 2017–2024) held that citizen-initiated versions need only a majority. It's prospective only — nothing already passed gets revoked.

## Why I land on No

1. **Minority rule.** This is the core problem. Under Prop 43, just 33.4% of voters can block a measure that 66.6% of their neighbors support. A supermajority check made sense as a constraint on *government* imposing taxes. But when the people themselves gather signatures and then vote to tax themselves at the ballot box, majority rule is the more democratic standard. The courts' reasoning — that the initiative power "coexists with, not displaces" Prop 218's restrictions — is sound.

2. **Real measures, real services would have failed.** Under the 2/3 threshold, these majority-passed citizen initiatives would never have become law: Sonoma County's wildfire/emergency-services sales tax (62%), SF's childcare tax (51%) and homeless-services tax (61%), Fresno's parks tax (52%), and LA's Measure ULA housing/homelessness tax (58%). Firefighters, teachers, nurses, the League of Women Voters, and the State PTA all oppose it — and all taxes of *special districts* (fire, hospital, library districts) are classified as special taxes, so those communities lose the most.

3. **It doesn't cut a single tax.** It just removes a funding tool while providing zero relief — and the fiscal timing is bad, with local budgets squeezed by inflation and federal health/human-services cuts.

4. **It's a hostage artifact.** Prop 43 exists only as the ransom paid to withdraw the Howard Jarvis initiative that would have *retroactively* voided taxes like LA's mansion tax. That initiative is already off the ballot — so a Yes vote buys you nothing from that deal. Even its own author, Assemblymember Buffy Wicks, is "adamantly opposed" to it, and Gov. Newsom, the Sac Bee, LA Times, and the state Democratic Party all urge No.

**The honest case for Yes:** if you believe taxation requires broad consensus, want parity between government- and citizen-proposed special taxes, and distrust the "council allies run it as an initiative to dodge 2/3" maneuvering, the Yes side is coherent. The Chronicle even declined to oppose it for that reason. But on the merits — democracy and practical local-services impact — **vote No**.
