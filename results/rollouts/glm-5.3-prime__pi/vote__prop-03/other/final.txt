I've now done a deep dive — the official voter guide and LAO analysis, the full legal text, the Berkeley IGS/CalMatters/LA Times/CalBudgetCenter explainers, the Stanford/Hoover research on high-earner migration, editorial positions from across the spectrum, and the latest polling. Here's what I found:

## What Prop 3 actually does
- **No new tax, no rate increase.** It makes permanent the existing top-bracket rates (10.3%, 11.3%, 12.3% vs. the 9.3% baseline) that voters approved in 2012 (Prop 30) and re-approved in 2016 (Prop 55, with 63% of the vote). Only the top ~2% of filers (roughly $371K+ single / $743K+ joint, inflation-adjusted) pay them. **A "no" vote is effectively a ~$59,000 average annual tax cut for top-1% filers** (ITEP estimate), ~98% of which goes to the top 1%.
- **Revenue: $5–15B/year** (about 5.5% of the General Fund; ~$104–120B raised since 2012). It flows through the constitutionally protected Education Protection Account (89% K-12 / 11% community colleges, banned from administrative costs, annually audited). Per the LAO, the *net* effect is ~40% new money for schools/colleges, with the rest freeing up General Fund capacity for Medi-Cal, reserves, and other services — so the "every dollar goes to classrooms" pitch is overstated, but the education benefit is real and large.
- **Downside of failure:** a $5–15B annual revenue hole starting in 2031, landing on schools (CTA estimates ~1-in-6 educator layoffs; LAUSD alone ~$839M/year) and a Medi-Cal system already absorbing big federal cuts, in a state running structural deficits.

## Key points from the research
- **Millionaire flight is mostly a myth:** Stanford/Princeton research on these very tax rates found migration explains ~1% of changes in California's millionaire population; after Prop 30, California lost only ~0.04% of affected filers. Even the Hoover Institution's more pessimistic study found departures among $5M+ earners rose only from 1.5% to ~2.1%.
- **Schools measurably improved:** California went from ~50th in per-pupil funding in 2012-13 to around the national average, funding universal TK, school meals, and the LCFF equity formula, while the rainy-day fund grew to cushion this revenue's volatility.
- **The "temporary means temporary" argument** is the strongest "no" case (OC Register, San Diego U-T oppose on this basis, plus complaints about unproductive spending and mediocre test scores). But the sunset is precisely what puts the choice back to voters — this is the third time they've been asked, and each renewal (2012, 2016) was approved. A constitutional tax provision isn't irreversible; voters can amend it again.
- **Breadth of support is unusually wide:** teachers, nurses, firefighters, PTA, Planned Parenthood, the Democratic Party, Newsom and Becerra — plus, notably, the billionaire-funded group fighting the Prop 40 wealth tax *endorses* Prop 3. Editorial boards are split but tilt yes (Sac Bee, SF Chronicle, Mercury News for; SD U-T, OC Register against). Polls consistently show 54–58% support. Campaign money: ~$81M for, ~$5.5K against.

## Weighing it
The "no" case is largely philosophical (broken temporary promise, high taxes generally, distrust of Sacramento) or about secondary issues (spending efficiency, test scores) that a $10B/yr revenue cliff would make worse, not better. The "yes" case is concrete: it preserves a 15-year-old, progressively-structured status quo that funds schools and health care, with the real-world evidence showing the tax has worked, the wealthy haven't fled, and losing the revenue would force severe cuts that would fall hardest on students and Medi-Cal patients.

**Yes**
