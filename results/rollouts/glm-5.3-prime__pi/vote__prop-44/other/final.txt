**No.**

Based on my research, I recommend voting **NO** on Proposition 44. Here's the evidence that drove my decision:

**What it would do:** Require ~2,000 nonprofit safety-net clinics (Federally Qualified Health Centers) to spend at least 90% of revenue on "program services," with penalties equal to the shortfall paid to the state (recoverable only if they comply within 5 years), plus criminal penalties for misclassifying expenses.

**Key reasons to vote no:**

1. **The threshold is set above the current average.** The nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) reports clinics currently spend an *average of ~80%* of revenue on health care services. A 90% floor — capping all administration at 10% — would put a large share of clinics out of compliance from day one. One analysis (opposition-commissioned, so partly self-interested) found 88–89% would fail in year one.

2. **It's dangerously vague.** The text doesn't define "program services" — that's left to the Attorney General, an elected politician, to decide what counts. Legitimate spending (nurse managers, IT, mammogram/X-ray machines, insurance enrollment help, translation) could be excluded, forcing cuts to comply, and misclassification carries criminal penalties.

3. **It targets an already heavily regulated sector.** FQHCs face federal HRSA oversight, mandatory audits, and governing boards that must be majority patients. They're nonprofits required to reinvest in their mission — this adds state bureaucracy (low tens of millions/year) funded by fees on the clinics themselves, at the exact moment federal Medicaid cuts are straining them.

4. **The coalition against it is remarkable.** Opponents include the California Medical Association, American Academy of Pediatrics-CA, Planned Parenthood, CA Academy of Family Physicians, ER doctors, OB-GYNs, school nurses, the Primary Care Association — *and* both the California Democratic and Republican parties. The only real backer is the union SEIU-UHW, which funded 100% of the Yes campaign (~$16.9M).

5. **It fits a familiar pattern.** This is SEIU-UHW's fourth industry-targeting spending-ratio measure after three failed dialysis propositions (2018, 2020, 2022), widely viewed as union-organizing leverage rather than genuine policy — a Fresno Bee report even noted the union has barely spent to promote Prop 44 this fall, and a PPIC poll found it trailing badly (34% support).

6. **Essentially no independent validators.** CalMatters lists zero media supporters; every major editorial board (SF Chronicle, LA-area, Sacramento/McClatchy, San Diego U-T, etc.) recommends No.

The pro side's underlying idea — more accountability for taxpayer dollars at clinics — isn't unreasonable, but this particular mechanism is poorly drafted, punitive by design, and risks closing clinics that are often the only care source in underserved communities. **Vote no.**
