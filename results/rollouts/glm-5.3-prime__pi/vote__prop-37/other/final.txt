**YES.**

Vote yes on Proposition 37.

After reviewing the official voter guide, the Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) analysis, PPIC's independent research, the Hoover Institution's policy breakdown, CalMatters' explainer, and endorsements/opposition across the political spectrum, here's why I landed on a yes:

**What it does:** Prop 37 lets the California Housing Finance Agency sell up to $25 billion in *revenue bonds* (not taxpayer-backed general obligation bonds) to fund second mortgages covering up to 17% of a newly built home's price for middle-income buyers (households earning up to 200% of area median income, putting down at least 3%). Buyers repay the loans, and those payments repay the bondholders.

**The decisive factors:**

1. **Minimal taxpayer risk.** The LAO—California's nonpartisan fiscal referee—says the measure has "no direct state or local costs." The legal text explicitly states the bonds are not a debt or pledge of the state's faith and credit. Even skeptics like the left-leaning California Budget & Policy Center concede: "if it doesn't work, the state's not really on the hook."

2. **The design targets supply, not just demand.** Unlike typical down-payment assistance (which economists warn just inflates prices by adding buyers to a fixed pool of homes), Prop 37 only works on *newly constructed* homes. It also includes construction-defect litigation reform designed to revive condo construction. This directly attacks the root cause of California's crisis: too few homes.

3. **Real upside for a real crisis.** California has the lowest homeownership rate in the nation. PPIC found 91% of renters are income-eligible, and full deployment could close roughly 40% of the state's homeownership gap with the rest of the country. The down payment—not always the monthly payment—is the binding constraint for many middle-class families.

4. **Asymmetry of outcomes.** If investors won't buy the bonds or buyers won't take the loans at unfavorable rates, the program simply sits unused—harmless. If it works, tens of thousands of families become homeowners. That's a favorable risk/reward structure.

**The honest caveats:** The League of Women Voters opposes it, arguing loan terms are unknown before the vote, the 200% AMI ceiling is generous (some eligible households earn $300k+), and it doesn't prioritize first-time/first-generation buyers. Some editorial boards (San Diego U-T, Santa Cruz Sentinel) argue it doesn't fix the underlying cost of homes. Those are fair criticisms—but they're critiques of *efficacy and targeting*, not of harm to taxpayers, and participation is entirely voluntary. Notably, no formal opposition argument was even submitted for the voter guide, and there's essentially zero funded opposition campaign, while support spans labor unions, realtors, the State Treasurer, State Controller, and multiple gubernatorial candidates across party lines.

Given a severe housing crisis, a thoughtfully structured supply-side subsidy, and near-zero downside risk to the public, the answer is **yes**.
