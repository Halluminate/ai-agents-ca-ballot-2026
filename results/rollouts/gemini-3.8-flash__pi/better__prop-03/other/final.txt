Whether it is "better" to vote **Yes** or **No** on Proposition 3 is a subjective political and value-based decision for each voter to make, as there is no objective or consensus answer. Both sides present distinct arguments rooted in differing priorities for California’s tax policy and public spending:

### The Case for Voting **YES**
* **Maintains Funding for Public Education and Healthcare:** A "Yes" vote makes permanent the tax rates originally enacted under Proposition 30 (2012) and extended under Proposition 55 (2016). Proponents argue that allowing these rates to expire in 2031 would result in a $5 billion to $15 billion annual loss in state revenue, threatening funding for K–12 classrooms, community colleges, and healthcare programs like Medi-Cal.
* **Taxes Only the Top Earners:** The measure applies only to the top 2% of income earners (single filers earning over approximately $371,000 and joint filers over $743,000, adjusted for inflation). Supporters argue it ensures wealthy residents continue paying their fair share without raising taxes on low- or middle-income Californians.
* **Accountability Protections:** The initiative includes constitutional guarantees that educational funds go directly to local school districts rather than state administrative overhead, subject to mandatory annual audits.
* **Key Supporters:** California Teachers Association, California School Nurses Organization, California State PTA, California Nurses Association, and Planned Parenthood Affiliates of California.

---

### The Case for Voting **NO**
* **Upholds the "Temporary" Promise:** Opponents emphasize that voters approved these higher rates with the explicit promise that they were temporary measures to resolve state budget emergencies. Voting "No" ensures that the tax increases sunset in 2031 as currently scheduled.
* **Concerns Over High Cost of Living and Taxes:** Opponents point out that California already has the highest top state income tax rate and high sales and gasoline taxes. They argue that making high taxes permanent harms the state's economic competitiveness and encourages high-income individuals and businesses to relocate.
* **Focus on Government Spending and Waste:** Critics argue that California faces recurring budget deficits due to runaway spending and inefficiency rather than insufficient revenue. They contend that Sacramento should rein in spending and reform existing programs before permanently locking in higher tax rates.
* **Key Opponents:** California Taxpayers Association, Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, Family Business Association of California, and California Hispanic Chambers of Commerce.

Because deciding between these competing perspectives depends on your individual economic philosophy, views on taxation, and priorities for state services, an AI cannot advise you to vote `yes` or `no`. Voters should weigh these arguments to make their own informed decision.
