### What Proposition 37 Would Do

Proposition 37 would authorize the California Housing Finance Agency (CalHFA) to issue up to **$25 billion in state revenue bonds** to fund a down payment assistance loan program for middle-income buyers of newly constructed homes.

* **Loan Structure:** The program would offer fixed-rate second mortgages covering up to **17%** of the purchase price of a newly built home. Homebuyers would be required to provide a down payment of at least **3%**, bringing the total initial equity to 20% (eliminating the need for private mortgage insurance).
* **Repayment & Fiscal Impact:** Because these are **revenue bonds**, they are repaid over time through the monthly mortgage payments made by the participating homeowners, not from the state’s General Fund. The Legislative Analyst’s Office (LAO) estimates **no direct state or local costs**.
* **Eligibility Limits:** 
  * Buyers must be California residents for at least one year, occupy the home as their primary residence, and have a household income no greater than 200% of the Area Median Income (AMI).
  * Eligible homes must be newly constructed or converted nonresidential properties priced at or below 125% of the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA) conforming loan limit (roughly $1 million to $1.5 million, depending on the county).
* **Developer Standards:** Developers can opt into a "qualified builder" track by adhering to specific skilled workforce and labor standards, in exchange for modified procedures regarding construction defect claims.

### What Your Vote Means

* **A YES vote means:** The state would be authorized to establish the middle-class homebuyer loan program and issue up to $25 billion in revenue bonds to finance down payment assistance loans for eligible newly constructed homes.
* **A NO vote means:** The state would not create this new homebuying assistance program, and the $25 billion in revenue bonds would not be authorized.

---

### Key Arguments for Voting YES

Supporters—including State Treasurer Fiona Ma, State Controller Malia Cohen, the California Conference of Carpenters, and organizations such as SPUR, the *San Francisco Chronicle*, and McClatchy editorial boards—argue:

1. **Overcomes the Down Payment Hurdle:** California has among the highest home prices and lowest homeownership rates in the country. Many middle-class workers (such as teachers, nurses, and firefighters) can manage monthly mortgage payments but cannot save the substantial cash down payment (often $100,000 to $200,000) required to purchase a home.
2. **Encourages New Housing Construction:** Because the program exclusively applies to newly built homes, it creates incentives to increase the state's housing supply rather than increasing competition for existing housing stock.
3. **No Burden on Taxpayers:** As revenue bonds repaid directly by the borrowers, the program carries no direct cost to the state's General Fund or local taxpayers.
4. **Consumer Protections:** Loans feature below-market fixed interest rates, strict caps on fees, no prepayment penalties, and mandatory annual financial audits.

---

### Key Arguments for Voting NO

While no official opposing argument was submitted to the voter guide, critics and editorial boards—including the *Mercury News*, *East Bay Times*, and the *Orange County Register*—raise several concerns:

1. **Higher Monthly Debt Burden:** Borrowing up to 17% via a second mortgage means buyers must make two loan payments every month, significantly increasing their ongoing housing costs and financial vulnerability to interest rate shifts or economic downturns.
2. **Broad Eligibility Limits:** The income limit (up to 200% AMI—reaching over $400,000 for a family in high-cost areas like San Francisco) and purchase price caps (up to ~$1.5 million) allow higher-earning households to qualify rather than targeting assistance to low- or moderate-income families.
3. **Not Limited to First-Time Homebuyers:** The measure does not strictly restrict the program to first-time buyers, meaning it could subsidize individuals who might otherwise be able to buy without state-backed financing.
4. **Changes to Construction Defect Rules:** The builder provisions alter existing defect litigation processes, which critics argue could weaken legal recourse and consumer protections for homeowners who encounter building defects.
5. **Treats Symptoms Rather Than Underlying Costs:** Opponents argue that adding debt-financed demand does not resolve the core driver of California's affordability crisis, which is the high cost and regulatory friction of home building.

### Conclusion

* If you believe the state should leverage revenue bonds to stimulate new home construction and help middle-income residents overcome the down payment barrier without direct taxpayer cost, you may lean toward voting **YES**.
* If you believe adding second-mortgage debt is the wrong approach, worry that the program's income caps are too broad, or object to the defect litigation trade-offs for builders, you may lean toward voting **NO**.
