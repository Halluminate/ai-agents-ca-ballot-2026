### What Proposition 4 Does

Proposition 4 amends California's Political Reform Act of 1974 to **repeal the existing ban on state and local governments offering public funding for political campaigns**. 

* **Current Law:** Since 1988 (following Proposition 73), California law has prohibited the use of public funds for political candidate campaigns, except in charter cities and counties that have established their own systems (such as Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Berkeley).
* **Proposed Law:** Prop 4 lifts that statewide ban, allowing state and local governments the option to establish public campaign financing programs. It does not automatically create a statewide funding system or mandate that local governments create one; rather, it permits them to do so under specific statutory guidelines.
* **Key Provisions & Safeguards:**
  * Prohibits using public funds earmarked for education, transportation, or public safety.
  * Requires participating candidates to meet strict eligibility requirements (demonstrating broad community support through small-dollar contributions) and agree to expenditure limits.
  * Prohibits using public campaign funds for fines, legal defense, or repayment of personal candidate loans.
  * Triples maximum fines for illegal campaign contributions from foreign entities.

---

### What Your Vote Means

* **A YES vote means:** State and local governments would be authorized to establish public campaign financing programs for candidates who meet qualification requirements and agree to spending limits.
* **A NO vote means:** The statewide ban remains in place; state and most local governments would continue to be prohibited from creating public campaign financing programs.

---

### Arguments in Favor (Voting YES)

* **Curbing the Influence of Big Donors:** Proponents argue that political campaigns in California are increasingly dominated by wealthy donors and special interest PACs. Public financing (such as small-dollar donation matching) allows candidates without access to personal wealth or big-dollar networks to run competitive campaigns.
* **Encouraging Grassroots Candidates and Diversity:** Supporters point to models in charter cities (e.g., Berkeley, San Francisco) and other states where public financing has helped a broader, more diverse pool of candidates enter politics and engage with local constituents rather than large financial backers.
* **Taxpayer Protections:** The measure includes explicit restrictions preventing funds from being drawn from education, roads, or public safety budgets, and sets rules to prevent candidates from using funds for personal enrichment or legal fees.
* **Local Control:** Supporters emphasize that the measure does not mandate spending; it gives local communities the democratic choice to adopt public financing if they determine it benefits their governance.

*Key Supporters include:* League of Women Voters of California, California Common Cause, California Clean Money Campaign, Consumer Watchdog, California Nurses Association, and various civic advocacy groups.

---

### Arguments Against (Voting NO)

* **Use of Taxpayer Dollars for Politics:** Opponents argue that taxpayer money should be reserved for essential public services and infrastructure rather than subsidizing political careers, negative campaign commercials, junk mail, and campaign consultants.
* **Lack of Specific Limits in the Text:** Critics contend the proposition creates a framework but leaves the specific funding mechanisms, candidate qualification thresholds, and total spending amounts to be determined by future legislation or the politicians themselves.
* **Does Not Stop Special Interest Spending:** Opponents point out that public funding does not eliminate independent expenditure committees (Super PACs) or outside political spending, meaning taxpayer money could simply be added on top of existing private and special interest money.
* **Fiscal Burden:** In times of state or local budget deficits, opponents maintain that even modest administrative and program costs are an unnecessary use of public revenue.

*Key Opponents include:* California Taxpayers Association, Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, Family Business Association of California, United Latinos Action, and former members of the Fair Political Practices Commission.
