---

### What Proposition 1 Does

Proposition 1 asks California voters to authorize **$11.25 billion** in state general obligation bonds for affordable housing and veteran programs:
* **$10 billion** for state housing programs, repaid from the state’s General Fund:
  * ~$7.2 billion for affordable multifamily rental housing and supportive housing for individuals experiencing homelessness.
  * ~$1.1 billion for down payment and homeownership assistance programs for low- and moderate-income buyers.
  * ~$500 million for housing infrastructure (water, sewer, roads, parks).
  * ~$450 million for farmworker housing.
  * ~$350 million for higher education student housing (UC and CSU).
  * ~$200 million for tribal housing.
  * ~$200 million for local cost-reduction housing pilot programs.
* **$1.25 billion** for the **CalVet Home Loan Program**, which helps veterans purchase homes. These bonds are repaid by the participating veterans through their mortgage payments, meaning this portion historically operates at no direct net cost to the state General Fund.

**Fiscal Impact:**
Repaying the $10 billion housing bond will cost the state General Fund an estimated **$500 million to $600 million annually for roughly 25 years** (about 0.25% of the state’s annual General Fund budget).

---

### Arguments in Favor (Voting YES)

* **Addresses the Housing and Homelessness Crisis:** Proponents argue that California faces an unprecedented shortage of affordable housing. The bond is estimated to fund or preserve up to 40,000 affordable rental units and assist roughly 40,000 households with homeownership.
* **Supports Vulnerable Populations and Essential Workers:** Funds are targeted toward low- and fixed-income seniors, people with disabilities, veterans, foster youth, farmworkers, and essential workers (such as teachers, nurses, and first responders) priced out of local housing markets.
* **Supports Veterans:** Dedicates $1.25 billion to expand the CalVet program, helping veterans secure affordable mortgages.
* **Leverages Matching Funds:** State bond funds often unlock billions of dollars in federal tax credits and matching subsidies that California would otherwise forgo.
* **No Direct New Taxes:** Bond funding does not raise income, sales, or property taxes directly, but rather repays debt over time from general revenues.
* **Key Endorsements:** Supported by veterans' organizations (US Vets, CalVet advocates), labor groups (California Federation of Teachers), affordable housing nonprofits (Habitat for Humanity California), and Governor Gavin Newsom.

---

### Arguments Against (Voting NO)

* **Adds to State Debt:** California already carries over $80 billion in bond debt. Opponents argue that committing $500 million to $600 million per year for the next 25 years strains an already volatile state budget and competes with other essential public services like education, healthcare, and infrastructure.
* **Does Not Fix Underlying Root Causes:** Critics contend that government subsidies and bond borrowing do not resolve California’s high construction costs, burdensome permitting processes, zoning restrictions, and environmental review (CEQA) delays that make building homes in California significantly more expensive than in other states.
* **Concern Over Cost-Effectiveness:** Some critics argue that state housing programs have had high per-unit costs and that taking on long-term interest costs (adding roughly 15% in total interest over time) is a costly way to finance construction compared to regulatory reforms.
* **Concerns Over Measure Framing:** While no official opposition argument was filed in the Secretary of State guide, several legislators and fiscal conservatives argued that the measure was branded heavily around veterans even though the majority of the funding goes to general state housing programs rather than direct veteran benefits.
* **Key Opposition:** Expressed by fiscal conservative organizations (e.g., Reform California), Republican state legislators, and independent editorial columnists concerned about state debt burdens.

---

### Conclusion

* Vote **YES** if you believe that investing state bond funds to subsidize affordable rental construction, homelessness solutions, and homebuyer/veteran loan programs is worth the long-term debt repayment costs.
* Vote **NO** if you believe the state should not incur additional long-term bond debt in a constrained budget environment, or if you believe housing policy should focus on reducing regulatory costs rather than bond financing.
