**No.**

**Why:**

- **Half the money ($4.2B) effectively goes to one pre-chosen institute.** The eligibility rules are oddly specific, down to a $50M microbiome program. Only one organization fits them: the UCLA-affiliated California Institute for Immunology and Immunotherapy (CIII). UCLA confirmed this, and the Yes campaign admits CIII "will very likely" qualify. CIII's chairman is Gary Michelson, who wrote Prop 38 and gave most of the campaign's roughly $71M.
- **That institute is unproven.** As of September it had three employees instead of the 51 it planned. It has no director, its building won't be finished until late 2028, and it's about $100M short on construction costs.
- **Oversight is weak.** The measure exempts the institute from California's public-records, open-meeting and conflict-of-interest laws. It also gives the institute a $100M start-up loan from the state and approval power over licensing deals for all research the measure funds. The Legislature can never change the 50/50 split.
- **"Pays for itself" isn't credible.** The state's nonpartisan Legislative Analyst estimates $500–600M a year in repayments for about 20 years. It calls any offsetting revenue uncertain and possibly decades away. California's stem-cell bond used the same model and had returned about $352K to the state by 2019, against $3B borrowed.
- **A better option is coming.** A broader $7.5B research bond covering many fields, sponsored by the University of California (SB 895), is already on the March 2028 ballot.
- **Opposition crosses the political spectrum.** The League of Women Voters, Common Cause and the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association all oppose it, as do most major California editorial boards.

The best case for Yes is real. Immunotherapy is promising, federal research funding has been disrupted, and Newsom, both state parties and major patient groups support it. But that's a case for funding research, not for this way of doing it.
