I know you asked for just yes or no, but I'm not going to pick for you on a ballot measure. After reading the bill, the Legislative Analyst's report and the arguments on both sides, I don't think the facts settle it. It comes down to how much you weigh housing help against caution about debt and cost, and informed people disagree. California's major newspapers split too. Here's a quick way to decide.

**Vote YES if you mostly agree that:**
- The state should pay to build homes for low-income and homeless Californians, because private builders won't build them at rents those people can afford.
- It's better to fund about 40,000 approved affordable units that are stalled for lack of money now than to wait for reforms that lower building costs.
- About $500–600M a year (roughly 0.25% of the state budget) is a fair price for buildings that must stay affordable for 55 years.

**Vote NO if you mostly agree that:**
- California shouldn't take on new long-term debt when it expects budget deficits of $20–35B a year.
- At $600,000–700,000+ per subsidized unit, adding money without fixing costs is poor value.
- The state can't be trusted to spend this well without stronger oversight.

**What it does**
- It borrows $11.25B:
  - $10B is paid back from the state budget.
  - $1.25B funds home loans for veterans, which veterans repay through their mortgages. So despite the name, only about 11% is for veterans, and that part pays for itself.
- About $7.2B goes to building and renovating apartments with income limits. The rest pays for help with down payments, housing for farmworkers, tribes and students, and related infrastructure.
- Repayment runs $500–600M a year for about 25 years, roughly $13–17B in total.
- The Legislative Analyst estimates up to 40,000 rental units, plus homebuying help for up to 40,000 households.

**Best case for yes**
- **The backlog is real.** The money from the 2018 housing bond was all committed by 2023. The main state housing program gets about $9 in requests for every $1 it has.
- **Each dollar goes further.** Housing groups say each state dollar brings in about $4 from federal, local and private sources. The units stay affordable long after the bond is paid off.
- **The state can afford it.** Debt payments are about 3% of state revenue, below the historical average of about 4%.

**Best case for no**
- **It's poor value.** RAND, a research group, found subsidized housing in California costs 1.5× as much per square foot as regular new housing, and the bond sets no cost limits.
- **It won't lower prices for most people.** 40,000 units is about 4% of the state's need for lower-income housing. Research also finds subsidized buildings partly replace private ones that would have been built anyway. CA YIMBY supports the bond but warns the $500M in down-payment help could push home prices up.
- **Oversight is weak.** The bill mainly requires an annual report, and the Legislature can move money between programs without another public vote. The State Auditor's 2018 recommendations for tighter oversight of the housing department are still listed as "no action taken."

**Who's on each side**
- **Yes:** Gov. Newsom, the state Democratic Party, the CA Chamber of Commerce, CA YIMBY, SPUR, the League of Women Voters, labor unions, big-city mayors from both parties, the SF Chronicle and the Sacramento Bee.
- **No:** Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, the California Taxpayers Association, the Libertarian Party, some Republican legislators, and the editorial boards of the Mercury News, OC Register, San Diego Union-Tribune and Press Democrat.
- The state Republican Party is neutral, and no one is funding a "no" campaign.
