**Yes.**

Prop 2 doubles the cap on California's rainy day fund, from 10% to 20% of General Fund taxes. It also saves more in stock-market boom years, keeps the required extra debt payments going until 2040 instead of 2030, and changes how savings count under the Gann spending limit. That limit caps state spending, and when revenue goes over it, the extra has to be split between taxpayer rebates and schools. I read the full text, the Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) analysis, the hearing transcript, and both sides' arguments. Here's why I land on yes, and the best case against.

**Why yes**
- **It addresses a real problem.** California's tax revenue is the 5th most volatile of any state, but its reserves rank 29th. The nonpartisan LAO estimated that current rules would cover only about a third of future budget shortfalls. The core of this measure (the 20% cap plus the Gann change) would cover roughly half.
- **It saves more of the windfalls.** It sets aside 150% of capital-gains revenue above 10% of General Fund taxes. The state Department of Finance estimates that would have saved about $4.7B more from 2019–22.
- **The Gann change shifts timing; it doesn't raise the limit.** Right now, money put into savings counts as "spending" under the cap, which discourages saving in boom years. Prop 2 counts it when it's taken out and spent instead. The LAO has called this "reasonable." Even the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, which opposes Prop 2, says this part "makes sense."
- **It doesn't raise taxes or change Prop 98 school funding.** Backers include the League of Women Voters, SPUR, the State PTA, the SF Chronicle and the Sacramento Bee. One Republican legislator, Josh Hoover, co-wrote an op-ed for it.

**The best case against**
- **It doesn't make the fund harder to tap.** The loose "budget emergency" rules stay the same, and they let lawmakers skip deposits and drain the fund even when revenue is at a record. The state has taken about $12B out over the past two years. The same budget deal that put Prop 2 on the ballot also skipped a $5.4B required deposit. Prop 2 even makes declaring an emergency a bit easier. A bigger cap only helps if lawmakers actually fill the fund.
- **Taxpayer rebates become less likely in boom years.** That, plus distrust of how the money will be used, is why the Jarvis group, Reform California, most Republicans, the OC Register and the San Diego Union-Tribune oppose it.
- **The required debt payments could go to more things.** They could pay back the state's own recent budget borrowing and its federal unemployment-insurance loan, not just pension debt. Prop 2 also requires half the set-aside money to go to debt until 2040. Under current law, that half would go into the rainy day fund by default after 2030.

**Bottom line:** Voting No doesn't make the fund any harder to raid either. It keeps the same rules with a smaller fund and less saved from boom years. Prop 2 is a step forward, not a fix; the LAO actually recommended raising the cap to 50% over time. If protecting taxpayer rebates is your top priority, or you think a bigger cushion just invites more spending, No is the consistent vote. Otherwise, I'd vote **Yes**.
