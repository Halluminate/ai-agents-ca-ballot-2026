I read Prop 45's full text, the Legislative Analyst's (LAO) analysis, and coverage from both sides, but I'm not going to pick yes or no for you. It comes down to how much you value faster building versus environmental review, and informed people split on it. Even Gov. Newsom, Sen. Scott Wiener, California YIMBY and SPUR (a Bay Area urban-planning think tank) stayed neutral, and they've all pushed to reform CEQA, California's environmental review law.

**What it does**
It creates an optional fast lane through CEQA for "essential" projects: all housing (including suburban subdivisions), water, clean energy and power lines, hospitals, schools, fire and police stations, wildfire work, broadband, and roads, highways and transit. It excludes jails, new oil and gas production, high-speed rail, the Delta tunnel and nuclear power. For projects that opt in:
- **Deadlines:** agencies get about 1.5 years to finish a full environmental impact report. If they miss that, the developer can force a decision and sue.
- **Less input:** public comment is capped at 45 days, and the developer can write the only alternative that gets studied.
- **Narrower lawsuits:** suits must be filed within 30 days and decided in about 270. They can only claim violations of "objective" existing laws. A judge can pause only the flawed part of a project, not cancel the approval.
- **Hard to change:** amending it takes a two-thirds vote of the Legislature, and changes must advance its purpose.
- **Cost:** the LAO expects state and local costs in the high tens of millions per year at first, possibly over $100 million. Long-term effects are uncertain either way.

**Best case for YES**
- The delay is real. A state oversight commission (Little Hoover) found CEQA lawsuits add almost two years on average. One Livermore affordable housing project lost two years to a suit the court called "almost utterly without merit."
- It applies tools that sped up housing permits, like firm deadlines and objective standards, to power lines, water and hospitals. It also covers housing that the 2025 infill exemption missed, such as sites over 20 acres.
- Other environmental laws still apply, and cities can still say no.
- Supporters include affordable housing builders, hospitals, clean energy groups, the NAACP's California/Hawaii chapter, Assemblymember Buffy Wicks, the state Republican Party, and most newspaper editorial boards, including the Mercury News and Sacramento Bee.

**Best case for NO**
- It goes well beyond deadlines. Environmental law professors at Berkeley and Stanford say it turns CEQA into a mere checklist for these projects. That would weaken protections no other law provides, such as for endangered species habitat.
- The scope is broad: freeway widenings, subdivisions on open land (including fire zones), dams and logging. Whether data centers qualify is disputed. The Legislature's own lawyers say the definition likely excludes them, others disagree, and the measure tells courts to resolve doubts in favor of approval.
- A 2025 law already exempted most urban infill housing from CEQA. Prop 45 has no affordability requirement, and its "$75,000 saved per home" figure comes from the campaign, not the LAO.
- If it misfires, it's hard to fix.
- Opponents include the California Democratic Party, the League of Women Voters, the Sierra Club, the American Lung Association, construction unions and the San Francisco Chronicle.

**How to decide**
- **Lean YES** if you think delays and lawsuits are a big reason California is so expensive, and you'll accept less review and a rigid law to build faster. That includes suburban housing and roads, not just apartments and clean energy.
- **Lean NO** if you think the 2025 reforms already handled the main housing problem, or you're worried about sprawl, habitat loss, freeways or data centers. The same goes if you'd rather the Legislature make narrower changes it can adjust later.

A quick gut check: which worries you more, a needed project stuck for years, or a harmful one approved before anyone fully studied it?
