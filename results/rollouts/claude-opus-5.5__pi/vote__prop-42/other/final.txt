I'm not going to pick for you on this one. It's a values question, and informed people disagree across party lines:

- **Yes:** Gov. Newsom, the state Republican Party, the firefighter, police and building-trades unions, and the San Diego Union-Tribune and OC Register.
- **No:** the state Democratic Party, the League of Women Voters, and the SF Chronicle, Sacramento Bee and Mercury News editorial boards.

But it comes down to one question.

**Do you want the state constitution to permanently ban new taxes on owning wealth, which would also block the billionaire tax?**
- Yes → **vote YES on 42**
- No → **vote NO on 42**

**How it plays against Prop 40 (the billionaire tax):**
- **You support Prop 40:** vote **NO**. If both pass, the one with more yes votes wins, and 42 is written to cancel 40 entirely.
- **You oppose Prop 40 and want a permanent ban on wealth taxes:** vote **YES**.
- **You oppose Prop 40 but don't want to limit future options:** vote **NO on both**. That's the Mercury News position. It said it could have backed 42 if it had only cancelled 40.

**What 42 actually does** (from the measure's text and the Legislative Analyst's Office, the state's nonpartisan fiscal analyst):
- Bans any new state tax on owning things other than real estate. That covers stocks, retirement accounts, business stakes, intellectual property, crypto, art and belongings. Taxes already in place before 2026 stay as they are, including income and capital-gains taxes.
- Bans most retroactive taxes, including taxes based on where you used to live. The only exception is a Legislature-passed tax that reaches back no more than a year during a governor-declared emergency, with the money limited to that emergency.
- Only another statewide vote could undo it.
- Fiscal impact, per the analyst: future revenue could be lower, by an unknown amount.

**Best case for Yes:**
- Wealth taxes are hard to value and run fairly.
- California relies heavily on a small group of top earners (the top 1% pay roughly 40–50% of state income tax), and they're the most likely to leave. A firm constitutional promise could slow that.
- It locks in the principle that tax rules shouldn't change after the fact.
- It leaves every existing tax alone. Supporters also argue that taxes aimed at the rich tend to widen over time.

**Best case for No:**
- It exists mainly to cancel Prop 40. Its $128M+ in funding comes almost entirely from a committee founded by Google co-founder Sergey Brin.
- The "protect your 401(k)" pitch is about a threat that hasn't shown up. Every past wealth-tax bill the Yes campaign cites targeted the very rich and died in the Legislature. Prop 40 itself exempts pensions and most retirement accounts.
- The ban reaches far beyond retirement savings. It also removes a common tool: making a tax start at the beginning of the year. For example, Prop 30 passed in November 2012 but applied to all of that year's income.
