**Recommendation: yes.** This is a judgment call on a policy question where reasonable people disagree. Here is why I land on yes.

**Arguments for**
- **Real projects are waiting for this money.** About 40,000 affordable homes in California are approved but stalled for lack of state funding. The 2018 housing bond is fully spent, and the 2026–27 budget has very little affordable-housing money.
- **It pulls in outside money.** Each state dollar typically brings $3.60–$4 from federal, local and private sources. A 2025 federal change made more federal tax credits available, so state gap funding is now the main bottleneck.
- **Borrowing fits what it buys.** The money pays for buildings that must stay affordable for 55 years, which is longer than the roughly 25–30 years of repayment.
- **The cost is modest relative to the budget.** Repayment runs $500–600 million a year, about 0.25% of the state's main budget. Total state bond payments stay at or below the historical average share of revenue.
- **Support is broad, including from business and pro-housing groups.** Backers include the California Chamber of Commerce, California YIMBY, SPUR and the League of Women Voters. The state Republican Party is neutral, and the Legislature passed it 61–7 and 29–2.

**Arguments against**
- **The debt is real.** Total repayment is about $17 billion over 30 years, and the state projects ongoing budget deficits.
- **Subsidized housing in California is very expensive.** Roughly $630,000–700,000 per unit, and the measure doesn't fix the rules that drive those costs up.
- **It won't change rents broadly.** Up to 40,000 rental units is small next to the roughly 1 million low-income homes the state says it needs.
- **Some pieces don't add new homes.** The $500 million in down-payment help could slightly push up home prices, and the money for buying existing buildings doesn't increase supply.
- **Results tracking is weak.** Past outcome tracking has been poor, and the Legislature can move money between the programs.

Opponents include the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, the California Taxpayers Association (CalTax), and the Mercury News, Orange County Register and San Diego Union-Tribune editorial boards.

Most of these objections argue for fixing costs alongside the bond, not for rejecting it. A no vote most likely means less affordable housing and unused federal money, not the same housing paid for some other way.

yes
