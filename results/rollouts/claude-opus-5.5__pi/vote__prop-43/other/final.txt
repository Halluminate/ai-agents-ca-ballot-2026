I won't pick yes or no for you on this one. The facts aren't really in dispute. What's left is a values question that splits along party lines, so I don't think the call should be mine. I can get you to your answer with one question, though.

**A tax set aside for one purpose (say, fire protection) gets on the ballot because residents gathered signatures, and 60% of voters approve it. Should it take effect?**
- **No, it should need two-thirds, the same as if the city council had proposed it → vote YES**
- **Yes, a majority should be enough → vote NO**

**A real example:** In 2020, Sonoma County supervisors put a fire sales tax on the ballot. It got 64.8%, which wasn't enough because it needed two-thirds. In 2024, residents gathered signatures for a nearly identical tax, and it passed with 61.7%. Supporters of Prop 43 call that a loophole. Opponents say it shows a clear majority getting what it wanted, after residents did the work.

**What it does**
- Today, a tax tied to a specific purpose (a "special tax") needs two-thirds if a council or county board puts it on the ballot. Court rulings since 2017 say it needs only a simple majority if citizens put it there by petition.
- Prop 43 would require two-thirds for citizen-proposed special taxes starting Jan. 1, 2027. That covers new taxes, increases and renewals.
- It doesn't repeal any existing tax (L.A.'s "mansion tax" stays). It doesn't change general taxes, which still pass by majority. It doesn't cap transfer taxes, and it doesn't affect anything on this ballot.
- The state's nonpartisan budget analyst says local revenue may be lower in the future, by an unknown amount.

**How it got on the ballot**
- It came out of a last-minute deal in June. The Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association withdrew a much broader initiative. That one would have voided some existing taxes and capped transfer taxes, and the analyst estimated it could cost local governments up to a couple of billion dollars a year.
- The Legislature passed this narrower version 35–1 and 68–2. Its author, Assemblymember Buffy Wicks, and Gov. Newsom now urge a no vote.
- The broader measure is gone no matter how this vote goes, so the deal itself isn't a reason to vote either way.

**Best case for YES**
- The same tax shouldn't need 50% or 66.7% depending on who gathered the signatures. Voters put the two-thirds rule in the constitution in 1978 (Prop 13) and 1996 (Prop 218), and courts created the exception.
- Petitions get used as a workaround. The Mercury News says this year's Bay Area transit tax went the petition route, funded by transit unions and contractors, to avoid needing two-thirds.
- It only applies going forward.
- **Backers:** Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, the California Taxpayers Association, the California Republican Party, the Mercury News, the San Diego Union-Tribune and the Southern California News Group papers.

**Best case for NO**
- About a third of voters could block what most want. Measures that would have failed include Sonoma's fire tax (62%), L.A.'s mansion tax (58%), San Francisco's 2018 homelessness tax (61%) and Fresno's parks tax (52%).
- The two routes already have different hurdles. A council can place a tax with one vote, while citizens have to collect thousands of signatures.
- Groups can still pass general taxes by majority. So Prop 43 may just push them toward taxes with no legal requirement on how the money is spent.
- **Opponents:** the California Democratic Party, Newsom, firefighters, teachers, the League of Women Voters, SPUR (a Bay Area urban policy group), the Sacramento Bee and the Press Democrat. The SF Chronicle took no side.

**Claims to take with a grain of salt**
- **Yes side:**
  - The "2,000 new taxes" figure isn't limited to the kind of tax Prop 43 covers.
  - The $54,000 home-sale example is misleading. San Francisco's 6% transfer-tax rate starts at $25 million, and Prop 43 doesn't cap transfer taxes anyway.
  - The claims that it "guarantees your vote" and forces governments to spend money as promised aren't in the measure's text.
- **No side:** "$3 billion a year" is the campaign's own number; the budget analyst gives no figure.
- **What's accurate:** courts, not voters, created the majority-vote route. And it's true that Prop 43 cuts no one's current taxes.

**Money and polls:** The yes side has raised about $15–16 million, about $10 million of it from the California Business Roundtable. The no side has raised about $6–10 million, mostly from unions. Recent polls show it losing: 42% yes to 55% no (PPIC) and 36% to 46% (UC Berkeley).

Answer that one question at the top, and you have your yes or no.
