I'm not going to give you a one-word yes or no here. How to vote on a contested measure is your call, and this one comes down mostly to values. The evidence doesn't settle it either way. Here's what the research shows, so you can decide quickly.

**What Prop 4 does:** It lifts California's 1988 ban on public campaign financing. It doesn't create or pay for any program. It just lets the state, counties, cities and special districts set up their own, with these rules:
- No money set aside for education, transportation or public safety can be used.
- Candidates who take public money have to accept spending limits and show broad support, such as a set number of small donors.
- Public money can't pay legal fees or fines, or repay a candidate's loans to their own campaign.

It also triples the maximum fine for illegal foreign donations. The state's direct cost is a few hundred thousand dollars a year.

**You might vote yes if:**
- You think big money is the main problem, and public money that matches small donations or gives voters vouchers is a fair counterweight. In Seattle, vouchers raised the number of donors per race about 350% and the number of candidates about 86%.
- You think every city and county should have the option that LA, SF and other "charter cities" already have. Supporters say California is the only state with a ban like this.

**You might vote no if:**
- You don't want tax money paying for campaigns, including ones you oppose, especially while budgets are tight.
- You want the details settled first. The measure sets no dollar caps or matching rates, the state's campaign watchdog won't enforce local programs, and the officials who'd benefit would write the rules. That's why the SF Chronicle says no, even though it likes public financing in principle.
- You find the evidence too thin. Studies are mixed on whether these programs reduce donors' influence. One major study found that legislators funded entirely with public money were more extreme. And nothing here limits outside groups or wealthy candidates spending their own money.

**Who's on each side:**
- **For:** Democrats, Gov. Newsom, the League of Women Voters, Common Cause, the ACLU, and the Sacramento and Fresno Bee editorial boards.
- **Against:** Republicans, the two big taxpayer groups (CalTax and Howard Jarvis), a former chair of the state's campaign watchdog, and the editorial boards of the SF Chronicle, Mercury News, OC Register and San Diego Union-Tribune.
- **Polls:** October polls show PPIC at 45% yes, 52% no, and Berkeley IGS tied at 41–41. Voters turned down earlier public-financing measures in 2000, 2006 and 2010, though each of those also set up a program.

**A quick test:** If you'd like your city or county to be *able* to try something like LA's or Seattle's program, that points to yes. If tax-funded campaigns are a dealbreaker however they're designed, or you want stronger safeguards written in first, that points to no.
