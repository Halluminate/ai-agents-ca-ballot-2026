I'm not going to pick a side for you on a partisan tax fight like this one. In yes/no form, though, it comes down to what you think about Prop 40, the billionaire tax:

- **If you want Prop 40 to take effect, vote No on 41.** Prop 41 is built to cancel it. If both pass and 41 gets more yes votes, Prop 40 is thrown out.
- **If you oppose Prop 40, it's a real judgment call.** You don't need 41 to beat 40; voting No on 40 does that. So 41 comes down to its own merits:
  - Vote **Yes** if you want every future state tax held to the 1979 state spending cap (the "Gann limit") and subject to required audits.
  - Vote **No** if you'd rather the state keep that flexibility.

**What Prop 41 does**
- Some ballot initiatives propose a "special tax," meaning the money can only go to a specific purpose. Once one of those campaigns has 25% of its signatures, the State Auditor must audit the programs it would fund. A summary goes in the voter guide, including ideas for cutting costs at least 10% a year.
- Programs paid for by special taxes passed on or after Jan 1, 2026 get audited again every 4 years.
- The state can't collect any tax passed on or after Jan 1, 2026 if its money is kept outside the spending cap. Under the cap, money above the limit for two years in a row gets split between taxpayer refunds and schools. Prop 40 is written to keep its money outside the cap, which is why the two conflict.
- It's a constitutional amendment, so only voters could change it later.
- The Legislative Analyst's Office (the state's nonpartisan budget office) puts audit costs in the low millions per year, rising over time and mostly paid from the new taxes. There could be some savings, and the overall cost is unknown.

**Best case for Yes**
- Voters would get independent information before approving new taxes for specific programs. Supporters point to a 2024 state audit that found about $24B in homelessness spending wasn't consistently tracked.
- New taxes couldn't sidestep a spending cap voters approved, so extra money would go to refunds and schools.
- The cost is small, and the 10% is a recommendation, not a required cut.

**Best case for No**
- Its main job this year is to kill Prop 40. The SF Chronicle wrote that "you don't have to support the billionaire tax" to see it as a poison pill.
- It permanently ties all future state taxes to a 1979 formula, making it harder to respond to things like wildfires or federal funding cuts. The Mercury News opposes Prop 40 and still opposes 41 for this reason.
- The audits mostly repeat work the State Auditor and Legislative Analyst already do, and the 10% target could make tax measures look bad in the voter guide.
- It might spill over onto taxes and fees that help fund Medi-Cal. The California Budget & Policy Center says that's possible but uncertain and would be up to the courts. The scarier claims, like a threat to Prop 3, come from the professors who wrote Prop 40.

**Who's on each side**
- **Yes:** CA Republican Party, CA Chamber of Commerce, CalTax, Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, the state CPA society, a former State Auditor, former Controller Steve Westly, and the San Diego Union-Tribune and OC Register. Almost all the money comes from a group funded by Google co-founder Sergey Brin.
- **No:** CA Democratic Party, the SEIU health care workers' union that put Prop 40 on the ballot, League of Women Voters (neutral on 40), ACLU SoCal, labor groups, SF Chronicle, Sacramento Bee and Mercury News.
- **Newsom:** no position on 41. He opposes 40 and supports Prop 42, which would also cancel it.

**Polls:** In late October, Berkeley IGS had Prop 41 at 38% yes, 47% no, and PPIC had it at 48% yes, 49% no. Prop 40 was also behind in both, and Prop 42 was polling better than 41.
