**no**

This is a judgment call, and the experts are split. The deciding factors for me:

- **Shaky revenue.** The state's nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office expects "tens of billions" collected over several years, plus a possible permanent loss of under $1 billion a year in income tax. Supporters say about $100 billion. A Hoover/Stanford team says about $30–40 billion, and a net loss of $25–39 billion once lost income tax is counted.
- **Serious legal risk.** The tax applies to anyone living in California on January 1, 2026, about ten months before the vote. The Tax Foundation argues this and other features make it unconstitutional. The drafters and their lawyers say it would survive. If courts strike it down, California gets the departures without the money.
- **Billionaires are already moving.** Page, Brin, Thiel and others left before the cutoff. Berkeley economist Enrico Moretti, who favors higher taxes on the very rich, found that billionaires move to avoid state-level estate taxes. He estimated California in particular would lose revenue from one, because its own income tax is so high.
- **One-time money for an ongoing gap.** The federal Medi-Cal cuts recur every year, so the revenue runs out with no plan for what comes after.
- **Weak spending safeguards.** The ban on replacing existing funds is undercut by another clause, so lawmakers could backfill existing health spending and free up general money for anything. Groups that would get the money oppose it, including the California Medical Association, Planned Parenthood Affiliates of California and the California Teachers Association. So do Gov. Newsom and the LA Times, SF Chronicle and Mercury News editorial boards.

The case for yes is real. The federal cuts could cost about 1.3 million Californians their coverage. Billionaires can easily afford 5%. Six Nobel economists back it. On the Analyst's Office numbers, the state would probably come out ahead on revenue. Zuckerberg has agreed with Meta's board to stay in California, and Nvidia's Jensen Huang says he's fine paying.

On balance, I think the measure is too likely to fail in court or backfire to be worth the risk, and better-designed ways to tax top earners exist. Polls taken in late October show it losing.

If you vote no on 40, how you vote on Props 41 and 42 doesn't affect it. If you vote yes, also vote no on both, because either one can cancel Prop 40 by getting more yes votes.
