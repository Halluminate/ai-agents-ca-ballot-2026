**No.**

The basic idea, making taxpayer-funded clinics put their money into patient care, sounds good. But the evidence says this measure would hurt well-run clinics too:

- **It punishes saving money.** The 90% rule is measured against a clinic's total income, not its spending. So any money a clinic sets aside for reserves, a new building or equipment counts against it, the same as overhead. For example, WellSpace Health spends 93.5% of its expenses on patient programs but would score only 77.9%, because it ran a surplus. With federal Medicaid cuts coming, clinics would have to drain their reserves or pay penalties.
- **Almost no clinic passes.** An independent analysis for the Sacramento Bee by Brad Fulton (Indiana University) found 89% of California clinic organizations would fail, owing about $1.6 billion in penalties the first year. Of the few that passed, nearly all were already losing money. The Legislative Analyst confirms clinics average about 80% and says some might close. The union told the SF Chronicle the 90% figure was partly modeled on a rule for insurance companies, not on clinic standards.
- **It's vague and risky.** The Attorney General would decide what counts as patient care, through bulletins rather than formal rules. Any patient could sue, and clinics couldn't defend themselves by showing they acted in good faith. Executives face possible jail time for misreporting. A federal judge said clinics can bring their federal-law challenge if the measure passes, so a court fight would likely follow.
- **The motive looks like leverage.** The union's earlier bill version (AB 1113) exempted clinics that joined a labor-management committee with the union. Clinics allege the union offered to drop the measure if 25,000 clinic workers were allowed to unionize. The union denies this.
- **Opposition crosses party lines.** Opponents include both the state Democratic and Republican parties, Gov. Newsom, the California Medical Association, pediatricians, ER doctors, Planned Parenthood, and every editorial board I found that weighed in. Supporters are essentially the sponsoring union and its labor allies.

**The fair point on the Yes side:** some clinics really do have troubling spending. AltaMed's CEO earned about $1.95 million, and a watchdog group has asked the IRS to investigate the organization. But Prop 44 doesn't target that well. AltaMed listed its art purchases as patient-program spending on its tax filings, so those might still count toward the 90%. Rules aimed directly at executive pay and transparency would fix that problem without risking clinic closures.
