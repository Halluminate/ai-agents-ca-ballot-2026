**Yes**, though it's a closer call than its 61% polling suggests.

**Why yes**
- **Little cost or risk for taxpayers.** These are revenue bonds that borrowers pay back. The Legislative Analyst found no direct state or local cost, and the bonds must state on their face that they are not state debt. If investors won't buy the bonds or buyers don't want the loans, the program mostly just doesn't happen. As the skeptical California Budget & Policy Center put it, "if it doesn't work, the state's not really on the hook."
- **Better designed than typical down-payment aid.** The loans can only be used on newly built homes, so new demand goes toward new supply instead of bidding up existing homes. California YIMBY stayed neutral, but called it "one of the better ways" to subsidize home buyers.
- **It includes a reform the Legislature couldn't pass.** Builders who accept certain labor standards get easier construction-defect lawsuit rules. Those lawsuits are widely blamed for suppressing condo construction, and a bill to change them died in the Legislature this year.
- **The need is real.** California's homeownership rate is among the lowest in the country. The Public Policy Institute of California estimates the money could help about 560,000 buyers if it goes mostly to entry-level homes.

**The best case for No**
- **The terms are unknown.** The interest rate is set only after the vote. The "below-market" promise depends on investors accepting roughly 3%. At normal market rates, the savings over FHA loans or mortgage insurance may be small. The campaign's 190,000-new-homes figure assumes that 3% rate, and even its author calls it the high end.
- **It's not targeted.** Households earning up to twice the area median income qualify, which is about $400K in San Francisco or Santa Clara. You don't have to be a first-time buyer.
- **Some provisions favor the sponsors.** Realtors put in about $20M of the roughly $33M raised, and every borrower must hire a real estate agent. The loans are also exempt from a 2025 California law that protects homeowners from abusive foreclosures on second mortgages, and the measure doesn't say why.
- **It adds risk for buyers.** Borrowers would carry a second monthly payment and owe 97% of the home's value. It could also push up new-home prices and steer growth toward far-out suburbs.

**Who's on each side:** Gov. Newsom, the Democratic Party, labor, the CalChamber, SPUR, the SF Chronicle and the Sacramento Bee support it. The League of Women Voters, Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, and the Mercury News, Orange County Register and San Diego Union-Tribune oppose it. The state Republican Party, the CalTax business group and California YIMBY are neutral.

**Bottom line:** Prop 37 will probably do modest good rather than fix the housing crisis. But the downside is limited and taxpayers aren't on the hook, so I'd vote yes. If you care most about helping the people who most need it, or you distrust measures written by the industries that benefit, No is a defensible vote.
