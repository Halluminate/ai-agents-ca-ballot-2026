I'll skip the one-word verdict on this one. Prop 3 comes down to values: how much top earners should be taxed and how much the state should spend. Voters split on it mostly along party lines, so I don't think I should pick for you. Here's what my research found.

**What it does**
- It removes the end date on the 10.3%–12.3% income tax brackets that voters approved in 2012 and extended in 2016. These rates only apply to income above about $371K for single filers or $743K for joint filers, roughly the top 2%.
- **Yes:** nothing changes on anyone's taxes. The rates become permanent and are written into the state constitution, so only another statewide vote could change them.
- **No:** the rates stay through the 2030 tax year, then drop to 9.3% starting in 2031.
- The state's nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) puts $5–15B a year at stake, rising and falling with the stock market. About 40% ends up going to schools and community colleges. The rest goes to Medi-Cal, other programs and reserves.

**Best case for Yes**
- It avoids a multibillion-dollar budget hole. The LAO already projects ongoing deficits, and federal cuts to Medi-Cal are coming.
- Letting it expire would cut taxes almost entirely for the top 1%.
- The rates have been in place since 2012. Over that time, the number of filers paying them roughly doubled and the economy boomed, so the predicted exodus of high earners hasn't wrecked the tax base.
- Supporters include teachers' unions, the PTA, the California Medical Association, Newsom and the Democratic Party. Newspaper backers include the SF Chronicle (which opposed the 2016 extension), the Mercury News and the Sacramento Bee.

**Best case for No**
- It was sold as "temporary" twice, in 2012 and again in 2016.
- It makes the state more dependent on its least stable money source. The top 2% pay about half of all income tax, which feeds boom-and-bust budgets.
- California already has the nation's highest top rate (13.3%).
  - A Stanford study found that people moving away and reporting less income wiped out about 45% of the 2012 tax's expected revenue in the first year and about 61% by the second.
  - Business owners who report business income on their personal returns also pay these rates.
- There's no deadline pressure. Voters could revisit this in 2028 or 2030, or pass another time-limited extension. Spending per student is already a record of about $27K while test scores lag.
- Opponents include the California Taxpayers Association, the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, the small-business group NFIB, the California Republican Party, the OC Register and the San Diego Union-Tribune.

**Rule of thumb:** Lean **yes** if your priority is keeping current school and health funding and having higher earners pay higher rates. Lean **no** if you care more about lower taxes, keeping the "temporary" promise, or the risks of budget swings and high earners leaving. No also fits if you'd rather push for spending reform first or decide closer to 2030.

**Context:** Late-October polls show it passing (58–40 in the Public Policy Institute of California poll, 54–37 in UC Berkeley's). The Yes campaign has raised about $81M; the No side has raised about $5,500.
