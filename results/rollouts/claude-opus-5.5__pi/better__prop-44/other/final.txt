**No.** Here's why:

- **Almost no clinic can meet the 90% rule.** An independent analysis by Indiana University's Brad Fulton, done for the Sacramento Bee, found 89% of California clinic organizations would fail in the first year and owe about $1.6 billion in penalties. Of the few that passed, all but two were already losing money. He wrote that "almost no health center in the U.S. meets Prop. 44's standard." The state's nonpartisan Legislative Analyst says clinics now average about 80%.
- **The math penalizes saving.** The 90% is measured against total revenue, not spending. So any money a clinic sets aside for reserves, equipment or a new building counts against it, even if every dollar it spends goes to care. That's a bad time to block reserves, with federal Medicaid cuts coming.
- **The penalties are very large.** One clinic with $106 million in revenue would owe about $10.6 million. Patients could also sue, and clinic boards couldn't defend themselves by showing they acted in good faith. What counts toward the 90% would be decided later by the Attorney General.
- **It looks like a bargaining tactic.** The union behind it, SEIU-UHW, sponsored three dialysis measures that voters rejected. Its 2025 version of this idea as a bill exempted clinics that joined its preferred labor-management committees. A lawsuit from the clinics alleges the union offered to drop the measure in exchange for union elections covering 25,000 clinic workers; the union denies this.
- **Opposition is broad.** Opponents include both the Democratic and Republican state parties, Governor Newsom, the League of Women Voters, doctors' and nurses' groups, Planned Parenthood and nearly every major California newspaper editorial board. Main backers are SEIU-UHW and the California Labor Federation.

The concern behind it is real: some clinic CEOs are paid close to $2 million, and some clinics have high overhead. But this measure is a poor way to fix that and risks cutting care for low-income patients.
