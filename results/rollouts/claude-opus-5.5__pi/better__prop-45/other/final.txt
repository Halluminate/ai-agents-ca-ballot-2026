I won't give a one-word yes or no on this one. After looking into it, the choice comes down to which risks and goals matter more to you, and well-informed people disagree. Even pro-housing groups that have pushed CEQA reform for years stayed neutral, including California YIMBY, YIMBY Action and SPUR. I don't think it's my place to tell you how to vote, so here's a summary to help you decide.

**What Prop 45 does**
- **Who it covers:** It creates an optional fast track through CEQA, the state's environmental review law, for "essential" projects. That includes nearly all housing (suburban subdivisions too), water, clean energy and transmission lines, hospitals, schools, fire and police stations, wildfire work, broadband, and roads, highways and transit. Related infrastructure is covered too. Jails, new oil and gas production, high-speed rail and the Delta tunnel are excluded.
- **Deadlines:** Agencies get about 1.5 years to finish a full environmental impact report. Lawsuits must be filed within 30 days and decided within 270.
- **Narrower review:** The developer proposes just one alternative to study, alongside the project and a "no project" option. Impacts are judged only against the rules in place when the application is filed. Public comment periods are capped. Only federally recognized tribes must be consulted.
- **Narrower lawsuits:** Courts can halt only the flawed part of a project, never cancel its approval.
- **Hard to change:** The Legislature would need a two-thirds vote to amend it.
- **Cost:** The state's Legislative Analyst's Office estimates early government costs in the high tens of millions to over $100 million a year, partly covered by fees. The long-term effect could be positive or negative.

**Best case for Yes**
- Delays and lawsuits under CEQA really do raise costs. Even Berkeley law professor Eric Biber, a sharp critic of the measure, agrees it targets real problems: shifting standards, endless re-studies, and lawsuits filed just to delay.
- It would speed up projects that the 2025 exemption for urban "infill" housing doesn't reach, like transmission lines, water projects, hospitals and big redevelopment sites.
- A study paid for by the measure's backers estimates about 20,000 more homes a year. The same study projects housing costs only about 0.7% lower after 10 years.
- **Backers include:** the California Chamber of Commerce, homebuilders, hospitals, the Farm Bureau, NAACP California/Hawaii, LULAC, clean-energy groups, Assemblymember Buffy Wicks and the state Republican Party. The Yes campaign counts 28 newspaper endorsements, including the Mercury News, Sacramento Bee and San Diego Union-Tribune.

**Best case for No**
- It's much broader than "essential" suggests. It covers freeway expansions, new subdivisions on undeveloped land including fire zones, and dams.
- It may also cover data centers. The Yes side cites a legal opinion saying it doesn't, but several law professors think it likely does.
- Environmental law experts at Berkeley and UCLA say it largely guts CEQA for covered projects, since the developer picks the alternative and courts can't stop a poorly studied project.
- Most urban infill housing was already exempted in 2025, so much of the added housing benefit would go to suburban sprawl.
- Mistakes would be hard to fix because of the two-thirds rule.
- **Opponents include:** the California Democratic Party, League of Women Voters, Sierra Club, American Lung Association, major labor unions and the SF Chronicle. Governor Newsom hasn't taken a position.

**How to decide**
- **Lean yes** if you think permit delays are a major reason California is so expensive, and you'll accept less environmental review and public input in exchange for faster building across the board.
- **Lean no** if you're more worried about sprawl, freeways, wildlife habitat, data centers or tribal input. Same if you'd rather the Legislature make targeted fixes that are easier to adjust later.

The latest statewide poll (PPIC, Oct. 16–22) had it at 46% yes and 50% no.
