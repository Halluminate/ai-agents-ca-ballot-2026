I'm not going to pick yes or no for you on this one. It comes down to how you weigh taxes against public funding, and voters split on it along party lines. I don't think I should put my thumb on that scale. But I did a lot of research, and here's a quick way to decide.

**The one-question test**
- **Vote Yes** if you'd rather keep today's top tax rates forever than have the state lose about $5–15 billion a year starting in 2031.
- **Vote No** if you think a tax that was sold as temporary should end on schedule, or at least shouldn't be written into the state constitution permanently.

**What it does**
- It removes the expiration date on the extra 1, 2 and 3 percentage-point rates on top earners. Voters first passed them as Prop 30 in 2012 and extended them with Prop 55 in 2016. Nothing changes until 2031.
- It's a constitutional amendment, so only another statewide vote could undo it.
- About 40% of the money goes to schools and community colleges. The rest goes to the state's general budget: health care, savings and other programs.

**Does it affect your taxes?**
Only if your taxable income is above about $371K (single) or $743K (married filing jointly). Those lines rise with inflation. Below them, nothing changes either way.

Rough extra tax per year from 2031, compared with letting it expire:
- Single, $500K: about $1,800
- Married, $1M: about $3,700
- Married, $2M: about $29,000

A one-time gain, like selling a house or business, can push you over the line for that year. For example, a couple earning $200K who sell a home with a $1.2M gain would pay roughly $1,500 more that year.

**Strongest case for Yes**
- The state budget already runs short. The June 2026 budget still projects shortfalls of $8–10B a year through 2029-30, and the Legislative Analyst warned of about $35B a year. Federal Medicaid cuts are still ramping up. Letting this expire would add $5–15B more.
- It only hits about the top 2% of earners. If it expires, the top 1% would get an average tax cut of about $59K a year.
- There's been no mass exodus. The number of filers in these brackets roughly doubled from 2013 to 2024, from about 185K to 376K.

**Strongest case for No**
- It was promised as temporary. Gov. Jerry Brown said so in 2012 and stayed neutral on the 2016 extension. The state's own analysis calls it making "an income tax increase" permanent.
- California already has the nation's highest top rate (13.3%). The best peer-reviewed study of Prop 30 found high earners' responses wiped out 45–61% of the expected revenue within two years, mostly because people who stayed reported less income. It also keeps the budget dependent on swings in the stock market.
- There's no rush. Voters could decide in 2028 or 2030 with more information.

**Claims to take with a grain of salt**
- Yes side: "15% school cuts, 1 in 6 teachers laid off" is overstated. Under the state's school-funding formula, schools would lose about 40% of the revenue, roughly 2–5% of school funding. The "strict accountability" rules aren't new; they carry over from Prop 30. The health-care benefit is indirect.
- No side: the claim that these rates now lose money comes from the same researchers' newer op-eds, not their peer-reviewed study. That study still found the tax raised money.
- Both sides spin it. "Not a tax increase" is true compared with today. Compared with current law, which lets the rates expire, it's a permanent increase starting in 2031.

**Who's on each side**
- **Yes:** Gov. Newsom, Democratic candidate for governor Xavier Becerra, the state Democratic Party, teachers' unions, the California Medical Association, firefighters, and the SF Chronicle, Mercury News and Sacramento Bee. Even the billionaire-funded group fighting the Prop 40 wealth tax endorsed it.
- **No:** the state Republican Party, Republican candidate for governor Steve Hilton, Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, CalTax, the San Diego Union-Tribune and the OC Register.
- Polls show 54–58% yes. The Yes campaign has raised about $81M; the No side about $5,500.

The best reads from each side are [the Hoover Institution's case against](https://www.hoover.org/prop-3-permanent-high-income-tax-initiative) and [the California Budget & Policy Center's case for](https://calbudgetcenter.org/resources/should-voters-approve-prop-3-how-top-tax-rates-have-helped-californians/). [The Legislative Analyst's summary](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=3&year=2026) is the neutral one.
