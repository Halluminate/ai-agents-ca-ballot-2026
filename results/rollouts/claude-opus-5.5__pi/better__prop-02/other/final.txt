On balance I'd vote yes: the measure mostly builds on the nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office's (LAO) advice, and the main objection, fewer possible taxpayer rebates, matters little in practice.

**For yes:**
- California's tax revenue is among the most volatile in the country, but its reserves rank only around the middle.
- The LAO estimates the current 10% cap would cover about 30% of future budget shortfalls. Raising the cap to 20% and changing the spending-limit rules would cover about 50%.
- Under the current spending limit (the Gann Limit), money put into savings counts as spending. That discourages saving in boom years. The measure counts the money when it is withdrawn instead, which the LAO called "reasonable" and had recommended.
- It saves more of stock-market windfalls and requires extra debt payments, which can't be suspended, until 2040 instead of 2030.
- Supporters include the San Francisco Chronicle, Mercury News and Sacramento Bee, SPUR, and the League of Women Voters. Bond analysts call it potentially credit-positive. Even the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, which opposes it, says the savings change "makes sense."

**For no:**
- Gann Limit rebates in future boom years become less likely. That has happened only once, in 1987, and the state is currently $27 billion under the limit.
- The broader list of debts the extra payments can go to could pull money away from paying down pensions.
- It leaves in place loose "budget emergency" rules. Those let lawmakers stop deposits or withdraw money even while revenue was rising.
- Opponents include the San Diego Union-Tribune, the Orange County Register's newspaper group, both main taxpayer groups, and the state Republican Party.

Most of the No case says the measure doesn't go far enough, not that it makes things worse than they are now.
