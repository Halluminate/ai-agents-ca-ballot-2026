

Based on my research, I've weighed the key considerations:

**What Prop 3 does:** It makes permanent the higher income tax rates (10.3–12.3%) on the top ~2% of earners (single filers >$371K, joint >$743K) that voters *already approved in 2012 (Prop 30) and renewed in 2016 (Prop 55)*, instead of letting them expire in 2031. It is not a new tax and raises no one's rate today.

**Why the evidence points to YES:**
- **Nonpartisan LAO analysis:** It maintains $5B–$15B in annual state revenue. If rejected, that revenue cliff hits in 2031 — the state would have to absorb it through deep cuts to schools, Medi-Cal, and other services, or raise other taxes affecting a far broader population.
- **The "No" scenario isn't fiscally neutral:** California's Prop 98 school-funding formula is tied to total General Fund revenue, so letting the tax expire would actually cut school funding *more* than the tax amount itself. The CTA warns up to 50,000 teacher layoffs could result.
- **Track record:** This revenue has funded schools through a recession, supported the rainy-day fund, and helped keep California's education funding from the bottom toward average nationally.
- **Endorsements:** McClatchy's California editorial boards (Sacramento Bee, etc.), Mercury News/East Bay Times, and UC Berkeley's Daily Californian all endorse Yes; major education, healthcare, and labor unions support it. Only taxpayer/business groups (which raised just ~$5,500 vs. ~$44M for Yes) oppose.
- **Polls:** 54–58% of likely voters plan to vote yes (PPIC, UC Berkeley IGS).
- **The No case's strengths** (revenue volatility, high-earner mobility, broken "temporary" promises, pension costs) are real but largely already baked into the current system's operation and LAO's estimates; they don't change the fact that a No vote triggers a $5–15B annual hole.
