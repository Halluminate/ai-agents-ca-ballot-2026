

**Yes.**

Here's the basis, from the official analysis, the Legislative Analyst's Office, and nonpartisan reporting (CalMatters, LA Times, IGS/Berkeley):

- **It's not a new tax for almost anyone.** It only makes the existing top-bracket rates (10.3%–12.3%, added in 2012 by Prop 30 and extended by Prop 55 in 2016) permanent. It affects roughly the top 2% of earners — those above ~$371,000 single / ~$743,000 married — and changes nothing for everyone below that.
- **The state's budget is already built on this money.** Voters approved it twice before, and it has delivered about $120 billion to schools and community colleges. The LAO estimates it generates $5–15 billion/year; if it's rejected, that revenue automatically disappears in 2031, forcing deep cuts (CTA estimates up to 50,000 teacher layoffs) and hitting Medi-Cal and other programs.
- **The accountability claims hold up.** The revenue flows through Prop 98 (40% of the state budget to K-12 and community colleges: 89% to K-12, 11% to community colleges), with annual audits and direct funding to districts.
- **The opposition is very weak.** The "No" side (mainly the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association) raised just $5,500 vs. ~$81 million for the "Yes" side, and the main "No" arguments (spending waste, brain drain) are contested rather than demonstrated.
- **Context:** California has among the worst class sizes in the nation (~22:1 vs. 15:1 nationally), and polls (PPIC: 58% of likely voters; IGS: 54% yes) show it's likely to pass anyway.

The honest tradeoff: "Yes" locks in the nation's highest top marginal rate (13.3%) permanently, and critics argue the state should fix spending before locking in taxes. But since the rate is already voter-approved and currently law, "Yes" preserves the status quo and avoids an automatic $5–15 billion/yr cliff, while "No" is effectively a future tax cut for the wealthiest 2% with immediate budget consequences for schools and healthcare.
