

**No.**

After researching this (Prop 43 on the Nov. 3, 2026 ballot — a legislative constitutional amendment that would require a two-thirds vote, instead of a simple majority, for citizen-initiated local special taxes starting Jan. 1, 2027, and would largely prohibit local property tax initiatives), here's the substance behind my "No":

**What it actually does:** It does not cut a single tax. It only raises the approval threshold for local *special taxes* (fire, schools, roads, transit, housing) when placed on the ballot by citizen initiative — a workaround that exists because of the 2017 *California Cannabis Coalition v. Upland* court ruling. Local governments proposing such taxes already must get two-thirds.

**Key considerations that swung me:**
- **It hands 33.4% of local voters a veto over 66.7%.** A measure backed by nearly two-thirds of your community would die. Several post-2017 initiatives (SF Measure C at 51%, Fresno Measure P at 52%, LA Measure ULA at 58%) show these barely clear a majority — almost none could ever hit two-thirds, especially in low-turnout local elections.
- **It blocks funding at a bad time.** Nonpartisan analyses (California Budget & Policy Center, SPUR) note counties and cities face rising costs and federal funding cuts (Medi-Cal/CalFresh) while this measure removes one of the few remaining local revenue tools.
- **The nonpartisan weight of opinion favors No:** the League of Women Voters of CA, both ACLU branches, Sierra Club, MALDEF, SPUR, all five major unions (CFT, CTA, CalFac, AFSCME, Nurses), and the editorial boards of the Sacramento Bee/Merit Media family all oppose it; CalMatters frames supermajority tax requirements as undemocratic. (Yes votes came mainly from HJTA, California Taxpayers Association, CalChamber, and a few Southern California papers.)
- **Even its sponsor opposes it:** Assemblymember Buffy Wicks, who wrote the bill placing it on the ballot, and the California Democratic Party back a "No" vote.
- The Legislature's own official title concedes it "limits voters' ability to raise revenues for local government services."

The "Yes" case is essentially an ideological taxpayer-protection argument (HJTA et al.) that the 2017 ruling created a "loophole" — but the practical effect is entrenching the nation's already extreme two-thirds local-tax bar against direct citizen action.

**Vote: No**
