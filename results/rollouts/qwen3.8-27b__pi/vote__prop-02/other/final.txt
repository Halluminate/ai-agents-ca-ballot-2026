

## YES

After researching the official voter guide, the nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's analysis, the California Budget & Policy Center's issue brief, and news coverage (CalMatters, LA Times, IJPR), here's my reasoning:

**What it is:** Prop 2 (the "Save for California's Future Act," Nov 3, 2026) is a constitutional amendment that doubles the cap on California's rainy day fund (Budget Stabilization Account) from 10% to 20% of General Fund tax revenues, requires larger savings when capital-gains revenue booms, extends mandatory debt paydown through 2040 (including California's ~$20B federal unemployment-insurance debt and pension liabilities), and exempts reserve *deposits* from the Gann spending limit (counting them only when withdrawn).

**Why I'd vote yes:**

1. **California's revenue volatility is extreme and demonstrated.** A ~$100B surplus in 2022 became a ~$50B deficit by 2023-24. In the last two fiscal years the state withdrew ~$12.2B from its rainy day fund and suspended $5.4B in required deposits to plug shortfalls — precisely the boom-bust whiplash a bigger reserve is designed to prevent.
2. **The 10% cap is already maxed out** (~$15.1B balance), so the state has stopped saving during a historic revenue boom. The 20% cap makes saving *mandatory* in good years rather than optional.
3. **It's a savings requirement, not new spending.** No new taxes; the nonpartisan Legislative Analyst found the only fiscal impact is higher reserves and more debt payments, and the LAO has long recommended the state save more than current rules require.
4. **The "cancels your tax rebate" argument is weak in practice.** The Gann Limit has actually forced a taxpayer rebate only once, in 1987 ($1.1B). Since Prop 111 (1990), it has rarely constrained the budget, and the last revenue "excess" (2020-21) was handled via one-time stimulus checks, not the rebate mechanism.
5. **Withdrawal rules aren't loosened.** The 2014 rules for when the fund can be raided stay in place, and federal cuts to Medi-Cal and food assistance raise the real risk of future deficits that a bigger buffer would cushion.

**The fair counterargument:** A larger fund could be raided by future governors (the 2024-26 suspensions show politicians will tap savings under a declared emergency), and the Gann-limit exemption is a genuine, if largely theoretical, weakening of the 1978 spending cap. But the practical case for a bigger mandated buffer in a state with this much revenue volatility — endorsed by the nonpartisan analyst, passed 59-8 in the Assembly with at least one Republican, and consistent with what voters approved in 2004 (Prop 58) and 2014 (Prop 2, passed ~70-30) — tips the balance.

**My answer: YES.**
