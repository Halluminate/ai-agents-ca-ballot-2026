

I've now reviewed the official voter guide, the nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's analysis, the full pro/con arguments, the CalMatters investigation, and California's current fiscal condition. Here's the picture:

**What Prop 38 does:** Authorizes an $8.4B general obligation bond for immunology/immunotherapy research (cancer, heart disease, Alzheimer's), repaid from the General Fund at **$500M–$600M annually for ~20 years** (~$12B total with interest).

**Key findings from research:**

1. **Half the money ($4.2B) is effectively earmarked for one institution.** CalMatters' analysis found the "qualifications" (200,000+ sq ft, UC medical center with 35,000+ inpatient admissions and 3.5M+ outpatient visits, specific $50M donations to a "microbiome research program," etc.) match exactly one institute in the state: the UCLA-affiliated **California Institute for Immunology & Immunotherapy (CIII)**. A UCLA spokesperson confirmed no other affiliated nonprofit could qualify. A ballot-process scholar called it "very clearly designed to support a specific nonprofit."

2. **Serious conflict of interest.** The proposition's chief payers — billionaires Gary Michelson, Meyer Luskin, and Michael Milken (who've raised ~$72M for the Yes campaign) — are the co-founders of CIII. Michelson is its board chair and its largest donor. In other words, the main funders would direct half of the borrowed public money to their own organization.

3. **The recipient is barely operating.** As of last month CIII had **3 employees** (vs. 51 planned), no executive director, no track record, most of its building not finished until 2028, and it would already get "primary responsibility" for negotiating sale/licensing of *all* drugs from the funded research — even those created by other institutions.

4. **The "no net cost to taxpayers" claim is contradicted by the state's own fiscal analysts.** The LAO puts the cost at $500M–$600M/year for ~20 years, with repayment offsets only *if* the research happens to produce revenue — uncertain, and possibly decades away. Notably, the LAO also notes borrowing costs ~10% more (inflation-adjusted) than paying cash upfront — and California has ~$100B in cash balances.

5. **Transparency gap.** The measure states the institute "shall not be deemed a public agency for any purpose under state law" — meaning the entity taking $4.2B in public debt money would be largely exempt from the California Public Records Act.

6. **Fiscal context:** California faces a structural deficit in 2026-27, with General Fund reserves being drawn down and a ~$29B "wall of debt." Meanwhile, a better-designed alternative (Sen. Wiener's $7.5B peer-reviewed research-foundation bond, SB 895) is already headed to the 2028 ballot.

**The fair "yes" case:** Immunology research is genuinely important and losing federal funding; major patient-advocacy orgs (Michael J. Fox Foundation, Alzheimer's Association, ALS Association) back it; the 20% drug discount for Californians is a real benefit; prior research bonds (71, 14) passed and produced results. But the science being good doesn't make this deal's design sound — the earmarking, the self-dealing, the records exemption, and the unsupported "free money" framing are serious governance problems.

**My recommendation: No**
