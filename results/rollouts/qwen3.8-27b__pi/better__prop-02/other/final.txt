

I've completed extensive research on this measure — California Proposition 2 (November 2026), the "Save for California's Future Act," a legislative constitutional amendment that would raise the rainy-day fund (Budget Stabilization Account) cap from 10% to 20% of General Fund tax revenue, require larger deposits during revenue booms, extend mandatory debt payments to 2040, and change how reserve deposits count toward the Gann spending limit.

Key findings from my research:

**For a YES vote:**
- The nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office has long said the current 10% cap is "stark contrast" to the scale of past recession revenue losses (~50% of General Fund taxes), and in 2025 explicitly said the state should build reserves far beyond 20% (gradually toward ~50%).
- California's revenue is extremely volatile (stock/AI-market dependent): a $100B+ surplus in 2022-23 followed by ~$50B deficits in 2024-25/2025-26, exacerbated by federal Medi-Cal cuts — exactly the boom-bust cycle a larger fund targets.
- Current reserves (~$17-20B after withdrawals) are far below what a typical recession would cost.
- SPUR, the California Budget & Policy Center, Sacramento Bee, and San Francisco Chronicle back it; the 2014 predecessor measure passed with ~70% of the vote.
- Extending mandatory debt repayment through 2040 saves interest on pension/retiree health debts and pays down the ~$20B federal UI loan — long-term money saved.
- The Gann-limit accounting change is economically neutral long-term (money counts when spent, not saved), and actually fixes a flaw where saving permanently ratchets up the spending cap.
- Withdrawal guardrails (budget emergency + legislative vote, capped withdrawals) are unchanged; the Legislature passed it 59-8/29-2, even with a Republican co-sponsor op-ed.

**Against:**
- Main opponent argument is that it makes taxpayer rebates less likely — but the Gann rebate has only been triggered three times in 47 years (1987, and twice as pandemic stimulus), and no rebate was issued even in the 2022-23 mega-surplus, so this is largely theoretical.
- "Slush fund" raiding concerns — real but unchanged from rules voters already approved in 2014.
- Opposition is concentrated in anti-spending-cap groups (Reform California, DeMaio, California Policy Center).

The nonpartisan fiscal consensus, the state's revenue volatility, the recent budget whiplash, and the interest-saving debt provisions all favor expansion of the reserve.
