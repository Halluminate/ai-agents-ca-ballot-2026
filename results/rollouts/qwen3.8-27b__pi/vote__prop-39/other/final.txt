

**No.**

After extensive research, here's why I'd vote no on this measure (Prop. 39, Nov. 3, 2026 — the voter ID / citizenship verification constitutional amendment):

**What it does:** Requires in-person voters to present government-issued ID every time; requires mail-ballot voters to write the last 4 digits of their designated ID number on the return envelope (ballots rejected if it doesn't match registration); mandates "best efforts" citizenship verification of voter rolls using government data with annual public county-by-county reporting; creates a free voter ID card; costs "tens of millions to low hundreds of millions" per year (nonpartisan Legislative Analyst).

**Why No:**
1. **It solves a problem that doesn't exist at any meaningful scale.** California already verifies voter identity at registration via DMV data (driver's license/SSN numbers). Documented voter fraud is vanishingly rare (Heritage Foundation: 193 proven cases out of 250 million mail ballots cast nationally 2000–2020). There's no evidence of widespread noncitizen voting in California.
2. **The citizenship-verification component is unreliable.** The only federal tool for this (DHS's SAVE system) is documented by ProPublica/Texas Tribune and courts to produce "ungodly" numbers of false positives — especially misidentifying naturalized citizens. A 2006 SSA audit found 7% of people classified as noncitizens were actually citizens; courts have found a "real risk" of erroneously removing voters. Mandatory annual public reporting by county would amplify this stigma and risk.
3. **Real disenfranchisement risk, concentrated on the vulnerable.** Critics (and the LA Times) note the measure does **not** explicitly require a "cure" process for ballots with missing/mismatched ID digits — in a state where ~90% of voters vote by mail, that's a huge new source of thrown-out valid ballots. Similar mail-ID rules in Texas saw mail ballot rejections spike, with Black, Latino, and Asian voters rejected at higher rates (Brennan Center). Studies show Californians who are lower-income, older, rural, disabled, or people of color are significantly less likely to hold current ID; "free" cards still require time, transport, and paperwork. GAO found voter ID laws cut turnout by ~2–4 points in Kansas and Tennessee 2008–2012 (research overall is mixed, but the risk is real and one-directional).
4. **Costly and permanent.** Tens of millions to hundreds of millions annually for speculative benefits, and as a constitutional amendment it's very hard to undo — with key details (acceptable IDs, "best efforts" definition) left to future legislatures.
5. **Context and polling.** The measure is funded largely by Trump megadonor Richard Uihlein ($17M) and pushed by Carl DeMaio as part of a national conservative agenda; it's opposed by the state's election officials' professional context (registrars say they already verify identity at registration), the ACLU, League of Women Voters, Common Cause, major labor unions, and the full Democratic establishment. The most recent high-quality polling (Berkeley IGS, Oct. 19–25) shows it trailing 57% No to 37% Yes, with opposition growing and independents leaning No.

The Yes side's case (a free ID card, keeping mail voting, audits, broad popular support for the abstract idea of ID) is not strong enough to offset the lack of a demonstrated problem, the flawed citizenship-verification mandate, and the new invalid-ballot risk in a mail-ballot state.
