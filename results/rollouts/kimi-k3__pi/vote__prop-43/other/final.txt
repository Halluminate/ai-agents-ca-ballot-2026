I've completed extensive research on this measure. Here's what I found:

**What Prop 43 does:** It amends the state constitution so that local special taxes proposed via citizen initiative (new, increased, or extended) would need two-thirds voter approval instead of a simple majority, starting Jan. 1, 2027. It also permanently bans new local property-value-based taxes. It doesn't cut any existing taxes.

**Key context:**
- This exists because of a 2017 court ruling (the *Upland* decision) that let citizen-initiated special taxes pass with a simple majority. Since then, ~two dozen measures (e.g., LA's Measure ULA "mansion tax," SF's Prop C homelessness tax) passed with between 50–66.6%.
- It was placed on the ballot nearly unanimously by the Legislature (35–1 Senate, 68–2 Assembly) purely as a compromise: the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association agreed to withdraw a far harsher, retroactive initiative in exchange. Notably, the measure's own author, Asm. Buffy Wicks, says she wrote it as leverage and doesn't want it to become law.
- Supporters: HJTA, business/taxpayer groups, OC Register, SD Union-Tribune, Mercury News. Opponents: CA Democratic Party, firefighters, teachers, nurses, SEIU, Sacramento Bee, Santa Cruz Sentinel. SF Chronicle took no position. Polls show it trailing (roughly 36–42% yes).

**My judgment:** While there's a real "level playing field" argument for closing the loophole, supermajority requirements let a one-third minority veto majority-supported funding for local services, and California's experience shows many popular measures (55–65% support) die under a two-thirds bar. The measure entrenches minority veto, restricts local self-governance further, and its own author disavows it.

**No**
