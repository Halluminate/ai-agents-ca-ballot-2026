I've completed extensive research. Here's what I found before giving my answer:

## What Prop 38 does (Nov. 3, 2026 ballot)
Authorizes **$8.4 billion in general obligation bonds** for immunology/immunotherapy research, costing the General Fund **$500–600 million per year for ~20–25 years** ($12B+ total with interest, per the nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office). Half goes to competitive research grants; **half ($4.2B) goes to a single unnamed institute** meeting pages of hyper-specific criteria.

## Key findings from independent reporting (CalMatters, STAT, LA Times, SF Chronicle, Sac Bee)
- **The criteria fit exactly one institution**: the California Institute for Immunology and Immunotherapy (CIII) at UCLA — right down to a 3.5M outpatient-visit threshold (UCLA has 4.1M; UCSF has 3.4M), a 200,000 sq ft requirement, and two specific $50M donations for a "rapid vaccine development program" and a "microbiome research program" that match gifts from CIII's own co-founder.
- **That co-founder, billionaire Gary Michelson, wrote Prop 38, chairs CIII's board, and funded ~$61M+ of the ~$71M Yes campaign.** The campaign's website doesn't mention CIII as the likely recipient.
- **CIII has three employees** (vs. 51 planned), no completed building until late 2028, a ~$100M construction shortfall, and no track record of bringing research to market.
- Prop 38 **exempts the institute from the Public Records Act, open-meeting laws, and the Political Reform Act** despite receiving $4.2B in public money.
- The "pays for itself" claim (10% revenue share, 20% CA drug discount) is **speculative per the LAO** — revenues are uncertain and could take decades; California's stem cell bond precedent (Props 71/14) produced minimal state revenue. The discount provision could also incentivize companies to sell elsewhere.
- The American Association of Immunologists' policy committee recommended **rescinding** its endorsement after learning of the earmark; it was kept only after the council met with Michelson.
- It locks in ballot-box earmarks for one field for decades while California runs structural deficits, bypassing the normal budget process.

**Editorial verdicts:** SF Chronicle ("one of the most noxious deposits on California's political dung heap in years" — NO), Mercury News/East Bay Times ("an $8.4 billion money grab" — NO), Sacramento Bee/McClatchy ("too big and too flawed" — NO), LA Times' Skelton (NO), plus a rare joint conservative/liberal op-ed against it. Supporters include Newsom, both state parties, and many patient advocacy groups — but the Yes campaign is funded almost entirely by the man whose institute gets half the money.

Funding medical research is a worthy goal, especially amid federal cuts — but this measure is a privately drafted earmark that hands $4.2B in taxpayer debt to its funder's own unbuilt, transparency-exempt institute.

**No.**
