## What this is

This is **Proposition 1 on the November 3, 2026 ballot** — the Veterans and Affordable Housing Bond Act of 2026 (SB 417, Cabaldon/Wicks). It authorizes **$11.25 billion in bonds**:

- **$10 billion** (General Fund-backed) for: $7.2B affordable multifamily rental housing (incl. supportive housing and preservation of at-risk affordable units), $1.1B homeownership/down payment assistance, $500M housing infrastructure, $450M farmworker housing, $350M UC/CSU student housing, $200M tribal housing, $200M cost-reduction pilot programs
- **$1.25 billion** for CalVet home loans — repaid entirely by veterans' mortgage payments, at zero net cost to the state (as the program has operated since 1921)

## Key findings from research

**The case for Yes:**
- California's prior housing bonds (2018 Props 1 & 2, the 2024 housing tranche) are fully spent, and ~46,000 shovel-ready affordable units in ~500 projects are stalled awaiting state subsidy. LAO estimates this funds up to **40,000 rental units + 40,000 households helped into homeownership**.
- Bond funds leverage federal tax credits and private capital, multiplying each state dollar; units carry 55-year affordability covenants.
- The cost — $500–600M/year for 25 years — is about **0.25% of the General Fund**, modest relative to the scale of the crisis (180,000+ homeless, majority of renters cost-burdened).
- This is genuinely *capital* spending (long-lived buildings), which is the textbook-appropriate use of bonds — unlike borrowing for operating costs.
- No organized opposition; no "No" argument was even submitted; passed the Legislature overwhelmingly (29–2 in the Senate).
- Endorsed by the SF Chronicle, Sacramento Bee, SPUR, League of Women Voters, veterans groups, and polling at ~60% yes (PPIC).

**The case for No (considered seriously):**
- Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Assoc. and the Southern California News Group editorial boards argue the state faces structural deficits, already pays ~$6B/year on existing bond debt, and that subsidized units cost $600K–$1M each — so this treats symptoms, not root construction-cost causes.
- LAO confirms borrowing costs ~15% more (inflation-adjusted) than paying cash.

On balance, the "no" critiques are valid about construction costs but don't offer a realistic alternative — the General Fund cannot produce $10B in cash amid deficits, and rejecting the bond strands shovel-ready projects, forfeits federal matching funds, and leaves homelessness costs (emergency services, shelters) on the public ledger instead. The debt service is small, and the CalVet portion is self-funding.

## My answer: **Yes**
